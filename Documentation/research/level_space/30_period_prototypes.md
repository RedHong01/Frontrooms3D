## 0. Short answer

1. **Every level has famous real prototypes from c. 1955–1993, and their drama is mostly sectional:** service floors between floors, galleries open below and sealed above, floors that terrace down a storey, stands built into a bank, pools sunk under roads and stands. This is the vertical skeleton `20` asked for, taken from real buildings and built from 1990 parts.
2. **Level 0.** Koolhaas's "Typical Plan" (1993) names its nature: "zero-degree architecture", an envelope, a core and a frame, so its checks come from the core itself (L0-P1, the open hoistway). Hertzberger's Centraal Beheer (1972) is an island field on exactly our grid (9 × 9 m islands of 3 × 3 m cells, voids, bridges), built "unfinished" for occupants to complete; Level 0 is that structure with the occupants missing (L0-P2, NEW F7). Rudolph's Yale A&A Building (37 levels in about nine floors) gives a split suite with unequal parts (L0-P3).
3. **Office.** The flat Bürolandschaft (1960) is the ground. Three office buildings give the checks: Willis Faber's escalator slot (1975), Lloyd's trading Room under open galleries (1986), and the Ford Foundation's L-shaped atrium, whose garden terraces down 4 m to a still pool (1967).
4. **The Office-to-Pool link is historical.** Willis Faber put a staff pool on its office ground floor, next to the computer and plant rooms; since 1994 it has lain, intact, under a false office floor (method only: NEW C8, the covered basin).
5. **Run!.** Hospitals and labs of 1960–72 gave the services "a floor of their own" (Salk, McMaster: walk-in service zones 9 ft deep) and planned hospitals as streets (Northwick Park). The moves keep the corridor a corridor: the services stay level while the floor ramps down, so the section opens toward the goal (RN-P1); a riser shaft opens overhead (RN-P2, NEW D7); thresholds mark wings of different dates (RN-P3); a racetrack loop for deep tiers (RN-P4).
6. **Pool.** Siza's Leça pools (1966) give the club's sequence: ramp down, a dim maze of changing rooms, then the water (PL-P1). The Oasis at Swindon (1976) gives leisure-pool grounds: a shelving lagoon, balconies, a dry beach, planter islands, a tunnel of unknown purpose (PL-P2). Munich (1972) and Montreal (1976) give a lopsided hall: a one-sided stand on a bank, and a pool under the stand (PL-P3, PL-P4).
7. **The well.** Most famous atria are symmetric; take their asymmetric parts. Hyatt San Francisco (1973): a triangle with two tiered sides and a zigzag. Kansas City (1980): walkways at three levels, one offset (its 1981 collapse is stated factually and never used). HSBC (1986): a glass floor over the void. Atlanta (1967): a glass car moving in the void. Walden 7 (1975): courts that do not line up.
8. **Joints.** The lobby can show the first view down through an escalator well, as Portman's Atlanta lobby does (J-P1). STAIR A can be one half of a 1968-code scissor stair, still honest and safe (J-P2).
9. **Four new terrain types** (§8): F7 island field, C8 covered basin, D7 riser shaft, E7 floor light.
10. **Each level keeps its nature.** Level 0 stays an untenanted office floor (empty islands, a core shaft, a split suite); Office stays an office (its atria are made of office floors); Run! stays a back-of-house corridor (no catwalk, no plant room); Pool stays a club and a pool.

## 1. Level 0: the untenanted office floor

Level 0 is the space-plan layer: a building-standard floor that never got its tenant (`10` §3). Its prototypes are the period's own theory of the typical floor, and two buildings that made one storey out of many grounds. Move names use a P (prototype) so they do not clash with `10`'s L0-1 … L0-7.

### 1.1 Rem Koolhaas, "Typical Plan" (essay, 1993; printed in *S,M,L,XL*, 1995) [LS120]
- **What it says:** "Typical Plan is an American invention. It is zero-degree architecture, architecture stripped of all traces of uniqueness and specificity." It runs from the loft through the RCA Building (1933) to the Exxon Building (1971) and the World Trade Center (1972–73). Business "makes no demands": the office is "the first totally abstract program" [LS120].
- Commentators reduce the typical plan to three parts: an envelope, a technical core and a load-bearing frame. In the speculative office the core gives access and services; the space between is indeterminate; partitions go in and out at will; raised floors and dropped ceilings carry the services [LS120].
- **Drama:** none, on purpose. The only fixed vertical things are the core's shafts.
- **Teaches:** Level 0 is the typical plan made literal. Its uniform field is right as the ground (`20` §1). Its vertical checks should come from parts the typical plan already owns: the core's shafts, the raised floor, the plenum.
- **Move L0-P1 · The open hoistway.** In the core (`10` L0-1), one elevator door stands open on an empty shaft: a 1-cell void, a timber barricade at 1.07 m across the door (E1), and the lit roof of a car two floors down in the stand-in shaft view. View-only (tier 1). It is the core every office floor has, so Level 0 stays an office floor. Codes: void cell + E1.
- **Era:** content (essay 1993; its examples 1933–73).

### 1.2 Centraal Beheer offices (Herman Hertzberger, 1968–72, Apeldoorn) [LS121]
- **What:** an insurance office for 1,000 people, built from one repeated unit: a 9 × 9 m square. A cruciform zone divides it into four 3 × 3 m corner work areas. Each unit is an "island". Bridges continue the arms of the cross to the next island. Where the space between islands is left open, it is a void "inciting vertical and diagonal contact". The stepped massing makes roof terraces. Four quadrants meet at a neutral zone of circulation, toilets and plant rooms; a technical tower is the highest point. Concrete-block parapets and standard furniture define the interior [LS121].
- The structure was meant to stay and the use to change; guides call it "unfinished" (*onaf*). Hertzberger's unfinished spaces "represent an invitation for completion and 'colouring' by the people who live there". Staff brought their own plants, pictures and furniture [LS121].
- **Drama:** a uniform field broken into islands by voids through several floors. Each island is seen from the next, diagonally, above and below. You cross only on bridges.
- **Teaches:** (1) Its unit is our grid: a 9 × 9 m island is 3 × 3 cells, four corner cells and a cross of five. (2) Its story is Level 0's: a structure built to be completed by its occupants. Level 0 is that structure with the occupants missing.
- **Move L0-P2 · The island field** (NEW F7, §8). One zone per floor at most. Islands of 3 × 3 cells separated by 1-cell void lanes open to the floor below (stand-in view). Each island has bridges (A6, 1 cell wide) on two or three of its four arms, never all four (R4), and never fewer than two (R6). Island floors step by +0.30 or +0.60 (A2, with B1 on the bridge). Block parapets at 1.07 m (E1, built like E2). No furniture: the islands are empty and the carpet and grid run on. The Relay crosses only on bridges, so the bridge count sets the chase; sight crosses every lane (R7).
- **Era:** content (1972).

### 1.3 Yale Art & Architecture Building (Paul Rudolph, 1958–63, New Haven) [LS122]
- **What:** 37 floor levels in seven storeys, a penthouse and two levels below grade (sources count nine or ten floors). Spaces pinwheel round four principal interior piers; a fifth shaft to one side holds the elevators and the main stair. Steps, balconies and bridges make "an ever-shifting internal topography". At the centre two double-height rooms are stacked: an exhibition hall ringed by an administrative mezzanine, and above it a two-storey drafting room crossed by two U-shaped concrete bridges and lit by skylights. Rooms range from dark "caves" (the basement auditorium) to "gold-fish bowls"; long diagonal views cross the building [LS122].
- After a fire in 1969 the drafting room was split into two floors; the 2008 restoration put the bridges back [LS122].
- **Drama:** section, not plan: many floor levels inside one storey height, double-height rooms seen from balconies, bridges inside a room.
- **Teaches:** split levels turn one floor into many grounds without leaving the building type. Rudolph's pinwheel is not symmetric, because every arm is a different room, size and height. `40` §1 rejected scene K (four equal quadrant platforms); the lesson here is unequal arms.
- **Move L0-P3 · The unequal Raumplan suite.** A Tall suite (5.4 m), about 4 × 5 cells, round two 0.9 m grid columns. Four parts, each a different size, level and lid:
  - a 2 × 3-cell "fishbowl" at 0, open to the full 5.4 m;
  - a 2 × 2 platform at +0.60 (A2);
  - a 2 × 2 "cave" at −0.90 (A3) under a low lid (D1, 2.4 m clear);
  - a 1 × 3 balcony at +1.80 on the cave's roof (−0.90 + 2.40 + 0.30 slab), overlooking the fishbowl (A5, E1), reached from both ends.
  
  B2 flights of 4–8 risers join them; one A6 bridge crosses the fishbowl at +2.70. Two ways onto every part (R6). Codes: F3, A2, A3, A5, A6, B2, D1, D5, E1.
- **Era:** content (1963).

## 2. Office: the furniture becomes the architecture

Office is the stuff layer (`10` §3). Its prototypes show the flat office field and the period's office atria. The flat field is the ground; the escalator well, the trading room and the asymmetric atrium are the checks.

### 2.1 The first Bürolandschaft: Buch und Ton, Bertelsmann (Quickborner Team, 1960–61, Gütersloh) [LS123]
- **What:** a test office on a converted warehouse top floor, about 128 × 220 ft (39 × 67 m) and nearly 10 ft high, lit by bare white fluorescent tubes; 270 workers in a concrete-frame building by Walter Henn [LS123]. Desks, punch-card machines, screens and plants stood in a "visually loose, even chaotic" arrangement, "as if a particularly untidy gang of burglars had ransacked the office" [LS123].
- The layout came from studies of who talked to whom. Paths were marked by plants and never ran through a work group. Sight lines were set so each worker could survey only part of the room. Machine noise masked conversations [LS123]. Osram in Munich (1963–65), Orenstein & Koppel in Dortmund (1964–65) and Ford in Cologne (1966) followed [LS123].
- **Drama:** none in section: one deep plane about 3 m high and 39 m across its short side. Its drama is a regular slab under an irregular layout that nobody can see whole.
- **Teaches:** the flat Office field is historically right (`10` OF-5). New here: the planned limit of sight is OF-1's refuge logic at the scale of the floor, and plant-lined paths that never cross a work group give the Relay readable routes.
- **Move OF-P1 · The deep field with planted paths.** An Office field up to 13 cells (39 m) from its window wall, with no bulkhead axis; 1-cell paths edged by planters at 1.1 m (E2) that turn at cluster edges; clusters sit off the column grid (R4). It stays flat on purpose: it is the ground that makes OF-P2 to OF-P4 count (`20` §1). Codes: A1, E2, E5.
- **Era:** content (1960–61).

### 2.2 Willis Faber & Dumas head office (Foster Associates, designed 1970–71, built 1973–75, Ipswich) [LS124]
- **What:** a low, deep-plan block of three storeys and a rooftop pavilion, built out to a curved site edge, for 1,350 staff. Columns stand at 14 m centres, perimeter columns at 7 m. An atrium through the centre holds three pairs of escalators in line under a tubular-framed rooflight; the escalators split the open first and second floors into two zones. Four service cores hold stairs and toilets; lifts are separate [LS124].
- The ground floor held a staff swimming pool, computer rooms and plant rooms; the top floor a restaurant in a turf roof garden. Raised floors with access panels carry power and cabling; suspended ceilings carry air, detectors and lamps with parabolic reflectors. Floors were green rubber (ground) and green carpet (above); partitions round service areas yellow [LS124].
- In 1994 the pool was covered over, not filled in, to gain office space; it remains visible beneath the false floor [LS124].
- **Drama:** a deep floor with few windows, cut once by a stepped escalator well that carries you in one diagonal from the pool floor to the roof garden.
- **Teaches:** (1) an office's own vertical event can be a long, narrow escalator cut, not a square atrium; (2) a staff pool on the ground floor of an office headquarters is a real 1975 programme, so the Office-to-Pool link is historical; (3) as method only, the 1994 floor-over: the pool is still there under the office floor (§4, C8).
- **Move OF-P2 · The escalator cut.** A slot of 1 × 4 cells (3 × 12 m) through an Office floor, off both centre lines, holding a pair of stopped escalators chained off at the top (E1). View-only: the floor below shows in the stand-in view. Variant (tier 3): the slot drops to a sunken field on the same floor (A3, −2.40) and one escalator is walkable, built as a B2 flight to the game's 0.15 / 0.30 module. Codes: void slot + E1, D5; variant A3 + B2.
- **Era:** content (1975); the 1994 floor-over is method only.

### 2.3 Lloyd's of London (Richard Rogers Partnership, 1978–86, London) [LS125]
- **What:** a rectangular atrium rises about 60 m to a barrel-vaulted glass roof. Floors wrap it as galleries (the "doughnut"). The Underwriting Room ("the Room") fills the lower four levels; all movement inside it is by a central escalator system. The first four galleries open onto the atrium; the higher floors are glazed in. Six towers stand outside the floor plate: three for fire fighting and escape, three for lifts, lavatories and risers, with plant rooms on top of four of them ("served and servant"). The ceiling lights double as air extractors [LS125].
- **Drama:** an atrium whose section changes with height: open galleries below, sealed glass above; escalators crossing the void.
- **Teaches:** the bottom of an office atrium can itself be an office floor, with galleries that belong to the same room. The servant zone outside the floor is Run!'s home (§3.4).
- **Move OF-P3 · The Room.** A Tall Office zone (5.4 m) of about 6 × 4 cells: a trading field on the floor (furniture: `62`); one gallery at +2.70 (A5) along two adjacent sides only, open to the room behind a glass guard (E1); a pair of B2 flights in escalator dress rises diagonally to the gallery; the second way up is a straight stair at the far end (R6). In the stand-in view, the floors above the gallery are sealed by glass fronts (E3). Codes: A5, B2, D5, E1, E3.
- **Era:** content (1986).

### 2.4 Ford Foundation building (Kevin Roche John Dinkeloo Associates, garden by Dan Kiley, 1963–67, New York) [LS127]
- **What:** a 12-storey glass cube. Offices line only the north and west inner walls (an L); the south and east sides are glass. The atrium is 160 ft (about 49 m) high; one source gives its area as 5,290 sq ft (about 490 m²). The garden follows a 13 ft (about 4.0 m) grade change between the 42nd and 43rd Street entrances. The main stair breaks into three flights whose landings open onto garden terraces at three levels; the terraces step down to a square still pool at the lowest level. Planters line the 3rd, 4th, 5th and 11th floors; the 10th and 11th storeys have balconies [LS127].
- The 43rd Street entrance sits at the atrium's northwest corner, so you enter high and see the whole atrium at once. Roche's first concept was an L-shaped building with a stepped section [LS127].
- **Drama:** asymmetric by construction: two sides offices, two sides glass; the floor itself drops a full storey across the atrium; you enter at the high corner and look down the terraces to water.
- **Teaches:** the Office stack (`20` V3) need not be a ring. Offices on two sides only, and a floor that terraces down to a basin, give an atrium with a front and a back, a top corner and a bottom corner. The still pool at the bottom foreshadows the Pool inside an office.
- **Move OF-P4 · The L-atrium.** An atrium of 4 × 4 cells through two or three Office storeys (D5, stand-in above). Glass office fronts (E3) on two adjacent sides only; the other two sides a lit wall of glass block or lowered blinds (sourceless, steady, never a cue). The atrium floor terraces down 3.60 m in three steps of 1.20 m (A4); a B2 stair breaks into three flights whose landings open onto the terraces; a second route runs down the far side (R6). A shallow still basin fills the lowest corner (C1, dry or wet). Enter at the high corner. Codes: D5, A4, B2, E3, E1, C1.
- **Era:** content (1967).

Already used as method elsewhere, not re-read here: the John Deere headquarters (Saarinen's office, 1964) behind *Control*'s rows [LS29], and Bell Labs, Holmdel (1962) behind *Severance* [LS28] [KNOWN, not re-read].

## 3. Run!: hospitals, laboratories and back-of-house

Run! is the services layer: a back-of-house egress corridor (B · Emergency Power, 27 m, painted soffit, sprinkler main, tray, conduit, glossy VCT). Its prototypes are the period's hospitals and laboratories, where the services were given "a floor of their own". Every move keeps the corridor a corridor: the floor, the soffit and the services change; no catwalk, no plant room (Red, 10-31).

### 3.1 Salk Institute laboratories (Louis I. Kahn, 1959–65, La Jolla) [LS129]
- **What:** laboratory floors alternate with full-storey "interstitial" pipe floors. Concrete Vierendeel trusses 9 ft (2.7 m) deep and 65 ft (19.8 m) long span the labs; ducts, pipes and conduits pass through the open truss webs, so the labs above and below are column-free. A perforated lab ceiling drops services on a 5 ft module [LS129]. After a first folded-plate scheme was dropped, the aim became to "give the pipes a floor of their own" [LS129]. Kahn called the two kinds of space "servant" and "served". Zoning put the lowest two floors below ground; light wells 40 ft deep and 25 ft square bring daylight down [LS129].
- **Drama:** a section in layers: a served floor, a servant floor 9 ft high, a served floor. The services are not hidden in a ceiling; they are a storey.
- **Teaches:** Run! is servant space, and its drama is how much of the section the services take. A 2.7 m service zone is an era-true depth.
- **Move RN-P1 · Level services over a falling floor.** The Run! corridor ramps down 1.5–3.0 m over its 27 m (B7: 1:12 runs with landings, or 1:8 as in `20` H). The soffit, the sprinkler main and the trays stay level. At the start you pass under services at 2.45 m clear (`10` RN-2); at the goal end they hang 4–5 m above you, and the full depth of the service zone shows from below. Compression turns into release inside the corridor's own section, at the goal door (`10` RN-4). R3: sink the floor and the room grows taller. Codes: B7, D6, D1 → D5.
- **Era:** content (1965).

### 3.2 McMaster University Health Sciences Centre (Craig, Zeidler & Strong, 1967–72, Hamilton, Ontario) [LS128]
- **What:** a four-storey hospital, medical school and research centre of about 1.76 million sq ft. Long-span steel trusses 9 ft deep make walk-in interstitial floors between the occupied floors, where horizontal services can be rearranged. Vertical service shafts are grouped round the columns; column-free modules are 73.6 ft (22.4 m) square. Functions spread round four elevator cores [LS128]. Glazed service and circulation towers, exposed trusses and ducts, and an automated materials-delivery system; inside, supergraphics and striped carpets [LS128].
- It was "intended to never be finished": service towers built for a fifth floor still rise above the roof, waiting [LS128].
- **Drama:** the services are a megastructure; the rooms are infill.
- **Teaches:** the vertical service shaft is the services layer's own vertical event, and a hospital shows it. Towers waiting for a floor that never came repeat Level 0's missing tenant one layer deeper.
- **Move RN-P2 · The riser chimney** (NEW D7, §8). In one cell of the corridor, never the first or last, the soffit opens into a 1-cell shaft 8–12 m tall. Risers, trays and pipe clamps climb out of sight; a caged ladder starts above reach; a battery head is fixed at each floor level up the shaft and stays lit after the trip. View-only: sight goes up, bodies do not. Codes: NEW D7, D6 at the cut.
- **Era:** content (1972).

### 3.3 Northwick Park Hospital and Clinical Research Centre (Llewelyn-Davies Weeks, planned 1962, opened 1970, Harrow) [LS130]
- **What:** John Weeks's "indeterminate architecture". A stable internal street network carries departments built as separate blocks, each shaped by its own function, from a seven-storey block to a low psychiatric wing. The hospital could grow by extending the street or the departments; department end walls were temporary, with removable fire escapes [LS130]. Vertical service voids recur at about 11 ft intervals [LS130]. Critics note long distances between departments, and a 6.9 m grid and 3.55 m floor-to-floor height that later blocked change [LS130].
- **Drama:** the street, not the rooms: one long corridor that every department hangs on, each at its own size, with ends built to be removed.
- **Teaches:** a real reason for a back-of-house corridor to change level and section along its length: each wing was built at its own time, grid and floor level, and the street absorbs the differences.
- **Move RN-P3 · The street absorbs the wings.** At each junction with a wing, the Run! floor changes by ±0.30–0.45 (B1 at a fire-door frame, or a short B7 run, as in T_RN_Thresholds) and the soffit steps with it (D6). Side stubs of 1 cell (inside the dead-end budget, `10` L0-7) end in temporary walls: painted plywood on studs with a stencilled extension notice (string: 叙事设计). The corridor itself always runs on to the goal. Codes: B1, B7, D6.
- **Era:** content (1970).

### 3.4 The racetrack nursing unit (1950s–70s) and Lloyd's servant towers (1986) [LS131, LS125]
- **What:** the racetrack (double-corridor) unit puts patient rooms on the perimeter and a core of utilities and circulation between two parallel corridors joined at the ends; 16–60 beds in UK practice [LS131]. The loop gives redundant circulation; the core's staff rooms are "dark rooms" that rely on artificial light [LS131]. Unit plans moved to crosses in the 1960s, circles in the 1970s and triangles in the 1980s [LS131]. Lloyd's put stairs, lifts, toilets and risers in six towers outside the floors (§2.3) [LS125].
- **Drama:** a loop instead of a line, round a windowless core you circle but never see into.
- **Teaches:** `10` RN-5 (the racetrack, later tiers) now has its precedent. A loop lets the hunter come from either side while the corridor stays a corridor.
- **Move RN-P4 · The racetrack** (deeper tiers only). Two parallel Run! corridors, 7–9 cells long, joined at both ends round a 2 × 7-cell core of utility rooms: doors shut and locked, rooms lit behind wire glass, never a hiding place. The goal door is on the outer wall, off the loop's centre line (R4). Codes: A1, B1, D6.
- **Era:** content (unit plans 1950s–80s; Lloyd's 1986).

## 4. Pool: natatoriums, leisure pools and club pools

Pool is the structure-and-site layer: a tenant fitness club on B2 that leads into a pool level (`10` §3, `bathroom_rooms/18`). Its prototypes are the period's competition halls, leisure pools, and pools inside office and commercial buildings. Slides: `61`; large stairs: `60`.

### 4.1 Leça swimming pools (Álvaro Siza, first design 1962, opened May 1965, completed 1966, Leça da Palmeira) [LS134]
- **What:** two pools set into the rocks of the Atlantic shore, below a coastal road. You enter down a concrete ramp parallel to the road. Rough concrete walls close off first the traffic, then the sea, so the ocean is only heard [LS134]. The changing rooms are a dark, dense space between two concrete walls, with black-stained pine partitions and roof beams, lit by thin gaps between wall and roof [LS134]. After them a path along a high wall leads back into the light, but the water stays hidden; then platforms open onto the sea [LS134].
- The adult pool is formed by low concrete walls joined to natural rock, its water level apparently the sea's. The children's pool has a curved wall on one side, rocks, and a concrete bridge at its entrance set low enough to discourage adults [LS134]. The 1962 design already separates barefoot and shod circuits, with level changes for footbaths; the 1965 drawings add platforms and concrete stairs between the levels of the sun terraces [LS134].
- **Drama:** a sequence in section: down a ramp, into a dim maze of walls, out along a wall, then the horizon. The pools are spliced terrain: built walls joined to found rock.
- **Teaches:** `10` PL-1's compression and release has a celebrated precedent built of walls alone. The club's changing rooms may be dark in tone without being dark (stained wood, not missing light).
- **Move PL-P1 · The descent to the water.** The club entry is a B7 ramp along the corridor wall (1:12, two runs, 1.5 m down). Then a maze of 1-cell changing corridors between tall walls under a low lid (D1, 2.4 m), where you hear water before you see it. A long wall runs you out sideways into the hall; only at its end does the deck open onto the water. The basin edge is spliced: low tiled walls join a column base, a slab edge and a stepped bank (A4), as if the water had found the structure. Barefoot and shod routes split at a footbath step (B1). Codes: B7, D1, B1, A4, C1, E1.
- **Era:** content (1966).

### 4.2 Oasis Leisure Centre (Gillinson, Barnett and Partners, 1974–76, Swindon) [LS135]
- **What:** a leisure pool opened on 1 January 1976 under a 45 m aluminium space-frame dome that rises 9.1 m above its ring beam, on a grassy berm round three-quarters of it [LS135]. The lagoon is irregular and shelves gently from floor level to 1.9 m. On the west side raised balconies with railings step down to the poolside; on the north is a circular "dry beach"; concrete planter beds carry tiled seats and fibreglass (GRP) rocks; clustered-globe lamp posts survive; a learners' pool sits behind a low wall; a wave machine made waves up to 1 m; there was a diving pool [LS135]. The entrance block has a restaurant and bar overlooking the pool, with the changing rooms below at pool level [LS135].
- Behind the learners' pool is a short tunnel; "its original purpose is unknown" [LS135]. Enclosed slides came in 1987 (`61`). The first of these "fun pools" was Bletchley (1974, demolished 2009): a free-form pool under a Perspex pyramid space frame [LS135].
- **Drama:** one round hall, but many grounds inside it: shelving water, raised balconies, a dry beach, planter islands, an overlooking bar, a tunnel to nowhere; on one side the pool sits below ground, under the berm.
- **Teaches:** the 1970s leisure pool is the period's own spliced terrain, inside a single symmetric shell. Take the inside, not the shell: R4 rules out the centred dome.
- **Move PL-P2 · Leisure-pool grounds in a basement shell.** In a Tall pool room on the 6 m column grid (no dome), placed off-centre: a beach shelving to −1.37 (C3); raised balconies at +0.90 and +1.80 on one side only, with steps down to the deck (A5, B2, E1); planter islands with tiled seats and GRP rocks (A7 dry, E2); a glazed bar at +2.70 overlooking the water (E3); a short lit tunnel of 2 cells (C6), open at both ends, that returns to the deck. Codes: C3, A5, B2, A7, E2, E3, C6, E1.
- **Era:** content (1976; slides 1987).

### 4.3 Munich Olympic Swimming Hall (Behnisch & Partner with Frei Otto, engineers Leonhardt + Andrä, 1967–72) [LS132]
- **What:** four pools enclosed by steel-and-glass walls on an irregular plan, under a cable-net roof covered in acrylic panels. A one-sided grandstand of concrete modules leans on the artificial embankment to the west; technical catwalks hang under the roof [LS132]. The architects formed the arenas as hollows, with ancillary rooms under the earth or under the stands; the roof "continues the movement of the ground" [LS132].
- **Drama:** an asymmetric section: the stand on one side only, built into a bank; the pools in a hollow; the roof dipping and rising.
- **Teaches:** a pool hall's section can be lopsided by its own logic: a raked bank on one side with rooms under it, open deck and glass on the other.
- **Move PL-P3 · The one-sided stand.** On one long side of a Tall pool hall, B6 seating steps (0.45 / 0.90) rise 2.7 m to a back gallery (A5). Under the stand, a low corridor of changing and plant rooms (D1) opens to the deck through gaps between the stand's modules. The other side has no stand: the deck runs to a wall of glass block. Two ways onto the stand: its end stairs (R6). Codes: B6, A5, D1, E1, C1.
- **Era:** content (1972).

### 4.4 Montreal Olympic Pool (Roger Taillibert, 1973–76, Montreal) [LS133]
- **What:** the aquatic centre sits at the base of the stadium's inclined tower. In 1976 it seated 10,000 (largely temporary; 2,777 today). A 50 m, five-lane training pool lay under the stands [LS133]. Today's pools: the 50 × 25 m competition pool, a diving pool with platforms at 3, 5, 7.5 and 10 m, a 50 × 12.5 m training pool, a deep-water pool with three 5 m levels, a warm-up pool and a wading pool [LS133]. The dates of the deep-water pool and of later high-diving platforms are [UNVERIFIED].
- **Drama:** a hall wedged under an inclined structure: low under the stands, high under the tower; the diving tower as a vertical object in the room.
- **Teaches:** a low pool under the stands, beside the tall hall, is real: compression and release in one section.
- **Move PL-P4 · The pool under the stand.** Under PL-P3's stand, a training channel one cell wide and 6–8 cells long (C4), under a raked lid that drops from 3.6 to 2.4 m (D1); its far end opens into the tall hall at deck level. Codes: C4, D1, D5 at the opening. The diving tower stays view-only and guarded (`20` §4.4).
- **Era:** content (1976); the deep-water pool and high platforms: method only until dated.

### 4.5 Pools inside office and commercial buildings: Willis Faber (1975) and Les Halles (1985) [LS124, LS136]
- **What:** Willis Faber put a staff pool on its office ground floor beside the computer and plant rooms; since 1994 it lies under a false floor (§2.2) [LS124]. In Paris, the Piscine Suzanne-Berlioux (Paul Chemetov and Borja Huidobro, 1985; one source says 1987) is a 50 m, 8-lane pool under the Forum des Halles, a shopping and transport complex. Large glazing on one side looked onto tropical greenhouses, replaced in 2016. A 1985 review called it "a grotto under Paris" [LS136].
- **Drama:** water in a building's lowest layers: beside the plant room, under a shopping centre, behind glass onto an indoor garden.
- **Teaches:** FrontRooms' premise, a pool deep in an office tower, has period precedents; their joints are the lesson.
- **Move PL-P5 · The covered basin** (NEW C8, §8). A room on the pool floor whose floor is a raised access floor on pedestals at deck level, over a full basin. Panels are missing in places and show water 1.2–1.4 m below (each hole guarded, E1); one open edge has steps down into the water (C7). The office floor literally stands on the pool. Codes: NEW C8, E1, C7.
- **Move PL-P6 · The garden window.** A long glass wall (E3) between the basin and an indoor planting court of the club, steadily lit; from the water the court is the visual magnet (`10` PL-3). Codes: E3, C1.
- **Era:** content (1975, 1985); the floor-over idea is method only (1994).

## 5. The well and stacked atria: hotels, malls and offices

The well is one void through every floor, from Level 0 down to the pool (`20` §3); Red found its first versions symmetric and ring-shaped (`40` §1). Most famous period atria are symmetric too. What to take from each is its asymmetric part.

### 5.1 Hyatt Regency Atlanta (John Portman, 1963–67) [LS137; deepens LS39]
- **What:** the first modern atrium hotel: a skylit court 22 storeys high, ringed by balconies "stacked tier upon tier", with all guest rooms reached from open balconies over the court [LS137]. Five glass elevator cars run in a shaft inside the atrium; the ground floor once held a lounge, trees and caged parrots; seen from above, a circular well in the atrium floor houses the escalators to the level below; a roof window shines down "like a moon" [LS137]. An architect's reading: Portman was "trying to get away from having these giant, dead-end corridors" [LS137].
- **Symmetric?** Yes: a centred court with equal rings. Take two parts: the lit car moving in the void, and a second well cut into the atrium floor.
- **Move W-P1 · The stopped car.** A glass elevator shaft rises against one side of the well, never centred. One lit car is stopped between two floors below Level 0. It stays at one world height while you descend, so you see it from above, then level, then from below: a depth gauge and a landmark for the whole game (Lynch, `10` [LS8]). View-only. Codes: D5, E3.
- **Era:** content (1967).

### 5.2 Hyatt Regency San Francisco (John Portman, 1971–73) [LS138]
- **What:** a hotel on an irregular, essentially triangular site at Embarcadero Center, shaped in height as a stepped pyramid [LS138]. The atrium, about 170 ft (52 m) high and over 90 m long, is bounded on all three sides by guest-room floors, tiered and open to the lobby. Two sides rise and narrow toward the skylight; the third is "a gigantic zigzag" of planes. Shops, cafés, bars and seating sit "at different floor levels" on the atrium floor, among trees, fountains and live birds [LS138]. Architects nearly always called it "Piranesian" [LS138].
- **Drama:** an atrium with no two sides alike: two tiered sides that narrow upward, one zigzag; a floor of several levels.
- **Move W-P2 · Raked, zigzag and sheer sides.** Seen from Level 0, two adjacent sides of the well step into the void by one cell every two floors, so their balconies read from above as descending terraces (A5, E1). The third side is a zigzag rim of 1-cell steps in plan (E1). The fourth is sheer, and carries the cascade (C5, `40` §7.5). The void's deepest point drifts toward the sheer side: an off-centre canyon, never the centred funnel of round 2. Codes: A5, E1, C5, D5.
- **Era:** content (1973).

### 5.3 Hyatt Regency Kansas City (opened 1980 [KNOWN, not re-read]) and the 1981 walkway collapse [LS139]
- **What:** three suspended walkways spanned the hotel's atrium at the second, third and fourth floors. The fourth-floor walkway hung from the roof framing; the second-floor walkway hung from the fourth, directly above it; the third was offset and hung independently [LS139]. On 17 July 1981 the second- and fourth-floor walkways fell to the atrium floor; the 1982 federal (NBS) report counts 113 dead and 186 injured [LS139]. It found the most probable cause to be the box-beam and hanger-rod connections: a weak original design, and a change during construction from continuous rods to two sets of rods, which roughly doubled the load on the fourth-floor connections [LS139].
- **What to take:** only the layout, not the event: bridges at different levels, each on its own line, layered when seen from below. The game never uses the collapse.
- **Move W-P3 · Offset bridges.** The well never shows two bridges stacked in plan. The one walkable bridge on a floor (A6, 1.5 m wide, E1 guards) has its own hangers to the slab above; bridges on other floors in the stand-in view run on different lines and at different angles. Codes: A6, E1, D5.
- **Era:** content (1980).

### 5.4 HSBC headquarters, Hong Kong (Foster Associates, 1979–86) [LS126]
- **What:** a suspended steel structure: eight masts in three bays carry floors hung from two-storey trusses. Three stepped towers of 29, 36 and 44 storeys give floors of varying width and depth [LS126]. Service cores sit at the perimeter, so deep-plan floors wrap a ten-storey atrium (sources give 40–55 m) above the glass floor of the banking hall; that hall is lifted about 10 m above a public plaza, and escalators rise from the plaza through the glass "underbelly" [LS126]. A 480-mirror "sunscoop" outside the south face and mirrors at the atrium top throw sunlight down to the plaza. The truss levels make double-height reception floors where high-speed lifts stop; escalators link the floors between, in "village-like clusters" (60 escalators in all) [LS126].
- **Symmetric?** The atrium is centred, but the section is not: stepped heights, a rhythm of double-height floors, a glass floor over the public below.
- **Move W-P4 · The glass rim** (NEW E7, §8). On one floor only, a 1 × 2-cell floor light of glass block projects from the rim over the well, walkable, inside the E1 guard line. You stand on it and see straight down to the lit rings below. Every third floor in the stand-in shaft is double height (the HSBC truss rhythm), so the shaft does not read as equal rings. Codes: NEW E7, E1, D5.
- **Era:** content (1985–86).

### 5.5 Toronto Eaton Centre galleria (Eberhard Zeidler with Bregman + Hamann, 1977–79) [LS140]
- **What:** a vaulted glass "interior street" 900 ft (274 m) long and 60 ft (18 m) wide, on several levels [LS140]. The mall sits on a gentle slope over a buried creek, so its north street entrance (Level 3) is one level higher than its south entrance (Level 2) [LS140]. When phase one opened in 1977, the temporary wall at the south end was mirrored over its full height to suggest the complete galleria [LS140]. Michael Snow's *Flight Stop* (1979), 60 fibreglass geese, hangs in the south end, seen from several storeys of wide balconies and the ground-floor corridor [LS140].
- **Drama:** a stacked void as a long slot, not a square; a floor that meets the city at two levels; a mirror that fakes the rest of the building.
- **Move W-P5 · The false continuation** (optional; needs mirrors, still an open pool-line decision, `10` PL-5). A Tall hall or the end of a long cut ends in a temporary wall mirrored over its full height, so the space seems to run on. Never on a chase route: glass and mirrors are never Relay cues (COMMON rule 12). Codes: E3 (as a mirror).
- **Era:** content (1977).

### 5.6 Walden 7 (Ricardo Bofill Taller de Arquitectura, 1972–75, Sant Just Desvern) [LS141]
- **What:** social housing of 18 towers, each displaced from its base, set in a curve so that they touch: "a vertical labyrinth with seven interconnected courtyards" (ArchDaily says five) [LS141]. The basic cell is a cube of 5.3 × 5.3 × 2.5 m with all services along one side; cubes are superimposed, juxtaposed and displaced into 14–16 floors (sources differ) [LS141]. In each courtyard, at different levels, bridges, balconies and galleries give access; the central courtyard holds the lift core; the displacements leave "huge overhead and side holes" [LS141]. Two pools sit on the roof; the courtyards are painted from celadon green to ultramarine blue [LS141].
- **Drama:** not one void but a cluster of courts, offset from floor to floor, crossed by bridges at many levels.
- **Teaches:** the well can be a family of voids that do not line up, rather than one square shaft (La Muralla Roja, by the same office, is `40` [LS41]).
- **Move W-P6 · The second court.** On some floors a smaller void of 1 × 2 cells opens beside the well, one cell away and turned; a 1-cell bridge (A6) between the two voids carries one route, and a loop elsewhere gives the second way round (R6). From above, the courts do not line up from floor to floor. Codes: D5, A6, E1.
- **Era:** content (1975).

## 6. Joints: the lobby and the stair

### 6.1 The lobby: entrances that show the whole building [LS127, LS137, LS126; `10` LS16]
- **What:** period lobbies stage the first view. The Ford Foundation's 43rd Street door opens at the atrium's high corner and shows everything at once [LS127]. Portman's Atlanta lobby has a circular well in its floor, with escalators down to the level below [LS137]. HSBC's escalators rise from the public plaza through a glass floor into the banking hall [LS126]. A 1990 spec tower spends its architecture on the lobby: 77 West Wacker's lobby is 42 ft high (`10` [LS16]).
- **Move J-P1 · The lobby well** (a suggestion to 游戏视觉; station look v1 is decided). In the lobby band next to the station (`10` J2), a 2 × 4-cell cut holds a stopped escalator pair going down to a dim concourse, guarded by a brass rail (E1). It is the player's first view down: the descent shown in the first room. View-only. Codes: void + E1, B5 for the lobby's own flared stair (`40` T_L0_LobbyStair).
- **Era:** content (1967–92).

### 6.2 The stair: the scissor stair of the high-rise code [LS142]
- **What:** New York's 1968 Building Code allows "two separate interior stairs … contained within the same enclosure (so called 'scissor stairs')", each separated from the other by construction of the enclosure's fire rating [LS142]. The 1968 code required the doors of scissor stairs to be at least 15 ft apart; the 2003 code asks for 30 ft or one third of the travel distance [LS142]. The code's stair section (as amended up to 2008) sets 44 in minimum width, 7 ft headroom, landings guarded at 3 ft, a 12 ft maximum rise per flight, and 2R + T between 24 and 25.5 in [LS142].
- **Drama:** two stairs braided in one shaft that never meet; long straight flights instead of a switchback.
- **Move J-P2 · STAIR A as half of a scissor stair** (optional, for 关卡设计 and 叙事设计). On each floor, a second stair door (STAIR B) sits at least 15 ft (4.6 m, so two cells) from STAIR A's, always locked; its flights cross STAIR A's on the other side of a solid wall. Nothing ever comes through it: the stairwell stays the one honest, safe space (`10` J1). STAIR A's flights may become long straight runs (B2) instead of the switchback (B3). Codes: B2 or B3.
- **Era:** content (1968 code).

### 9.1 Limits and doubts

- Sources disagree on: the Yale A&A floor count (nine or ten); the HSBC atrium height (40–55 m); Walden 7's floors (14–16) and courts (five or seven); the Les Halles pool's opening (1985 or 1987); the Kansas City toll (113 dead in the 1982 report; later counts often say 114 [UNVERIFIED]).
- Koolhaas's essay is read through an OCR excerpt and commentaries, not the book [LS120]. The racetrack history and the Montreal pool list come from secondary or later sources [LS131, LS133]; Montreal's deep-water pool and high platforms are undated [UNVERIFIED].
- The NYC stair numbers are the 1968 code as amended to 2008; only the 15 ft scissor-door rule is confirmed as the 1968 text (NIST) [LS142].
- Pruned without research: Yoyogi National Gymnasium (1964; a point-symmetric arena), Habitat 67 (housing), Le Corbusier's Venice Hospital (unbuilt), corporate postmodern lobbies beyond 77 West Wacker. Bell Labs and John Deere stay as LS28 and LS29.
- Dimensions inside the moves (cells, risers, heights) are design numbers on the 3 m grid, not sourced facts. Moves are proposals; owners as in `10` §1.2.
