# Level space · 31 · Contemporary spatial concepts (2010–2026): method for FrontRooms

Date: 2026-10-31. Written by a research sub-session of the K session (Cursor) for line bathroom.

Red, 10-31 (verbatim): "...然后还有所有最新的，专注于空间的一流概念设计（可以涉及到这些题材比如医院泳池办公楼酒店等，但是也可以稍微拓宽一些，只借鉴空间设计）". This page is direction 2: the latest first-rate, space-focused concept and spatial design, roughly 2010–2026.

Builds on, does not repeat: `10_research.md` §0–3 (one building, one layer per level), `20_structural_drama.md` §0–5 (vertical devices, the well, legibility), `40_terrain_grammar.md` §0, §4, §5.3, §7 (types A–F, rules R1–R8), `50_refinement.md` §0 (B4-S/M/L; the Relay never uses them). Not covered here: large feature stairs (`60`), slides (`61`), furniture (`62`, `63`). Sources [LS150]–[LS177] in §6. Research only: no code, no Unity, no download; every work is post-era and used as method only.

## 0. Short answer

1. **The best recent spatial design is sectional, not spectacular.** Its drama comes from one lid over many grounds (*Severance* S2's Mammalians room, Maggie's Gartnavel), a lid that becomes the horizon (KAIT Plaza), and floors that slope or step so the room changes under you (Rolex Learning Center, the Seattle Books Spiral) [LS151, LS167–LS169, LS174]. Symmetric showpieces (Vessel, the *Dune* tent, *Tenet*'s mirrored room) give one idea each, and R4 breaks them.
2. **One lid, many grounds (F8, NEW).** Keep Level 0's building-standard grid flat and step the floor beneath it from −1.20 to +0.60. Headroom runs 4.10 → 2.30 m; on the dais the Relay is 0.25 m from the ceiling. The space plan going wrong inside one room. Level 0 and Office (§1.1).
3. **The lid as horizon (F9, NEW).** A Level 0 pit whose ceiling steps down with the floor: from one rim, the far rim shows only up to about 1.7 m. The Relay is cut by the ceiling, never hidden by darkness (§3.2).
4. **The slot, not the square.** Depot Boijmans's atrium is 40 m high but only 6 m deep, crossed by zigzag flights and lined with storage windows [LS170]. A 2-cell slot stair hall for Office (F1b), and the well as a 2 × 5 slot (option), cannot be ringed symmetrically: a direct answer to Red's 16:06 note (§3.5).
5. **Back-of-house made visible, Run! kept a corridor (E8, NEW).** Two bays of chain-link storage cages along the spine: sight and sound cross, bodies do not. Plus lit thresholds after the trip, fire-door vision panels that show the next stretch already tripped (*Tenet*), and services in strata thickening toward the dock (*Arrival*) (§1.3, §1.7, §2.1).
6. **The pool as blocks, not a box (C9, NEW).** Therme Vals puts small rooms inside solid blocks and makes the pool the space between [LS172]. The club's whirlpool, steam room, plunge and sauna become tiled blocks pinwheeling in the hall, entered from a platform and a long ramp. With them: the audible island (*Blade Runner 2049*), the lid that divides the hall (London Aquatics Centre), the stepwell wall on one side only (Vessel), the curved glass-block passage (Elbphilharmonie) (§1.2, §3.6–§3.8, §3.11).
7. **Office: furniture as terrain.** A blade field of freestanding panels that is a wall one way and open the other (KAIT Workshop); a creeping 2° slope under the flat grid (B9, NEW); one aligned line of openings through four layers of shelving (Fujimoto) (§3.1, §3.4, §3.10).
8. **Games give rules a player can keep.** *Alan Wake 2*'s prototype failed on flat crossings and too much darkness; the fix was an elevated plaza that sees every key place, and V-forks [LS161]. *Cocoon* frames everything needed in the landing view; *Prey* puts the exit across the room, in sight and lit [LS162, LS165]. For us: forks in Level 0, an overlook deck in Pool, an arrival frame at every STAIR A door (§2).
9. **Method from film.** Design the building's process before the corridor (*Andor*); a stair hidden behind storeroom shelving to a floor under the lowest floor (*Parasite*) for the club's back room; a stair maze of straight flights only (*Squid Game*) for the deepest rooms (§1.5, §1.6, §1.8).
10. **Guards kept.** Post-era works give method only; no composition is copied; no move needs a spiral, so the Relay can follow every route. Two things are explicitly not taken: *Control: AWE*'s dark rooms and Vals's dark "sounding stone" (darkness is never cover). Each level keeps its nature.

## 1. Film and TV production design

Era guard for the whole page: every work here is post-era. It gives method and spatial ideas only. Every move below is built from 1990 building parts, keeps the level's nature, and uses the codes of `40` §4 (A–F) and rules R1–R8 (`40` §5.3).

### 1.1 *Severance* season 2 (Apple TV+, 2025). Production designer Jeremy Hindle

Season 1 is LS28 (the jogging hallways, narrow near the elevator, wider deeper). New in season 2:
- **The Mammalians room.** Hindle read the word and saw "a football field underground with rolling hills" [LS150]. It was shot with real grass for the goats inside a tent with no centre supports, 40 ft high at the centre, from a company that builds such tents up to 1,000 ft long and 140 ft wide [LS151].
- **One flat lid over an uneven ground.** The finished room has a dropped ceiling about 25 ft up (built about 10 ft high on set, the rest CG). Hindle: "I picked the highest point for the tallest actor", so that she stands "inches away from the ceiling": "we're low and then all of a sudden we're right there with her" [LS151]. The fixtures were split into separate lamps with gaps between them, not one continuous band [LS151].
- **The experiment rooms.** "Every angle's a little bit off… the table she sits at is a triangle. It's the sharpest point coming out to you… low ceilings always help" [LS150].
- **Depth as an open question.** Hindle asked the creator how far underground Lumon goes: "Oh, miles" [LS150]. The hallway stages were "massive" and "never big enough"; the season-2 opening run was extended with a CG copy of the practical hallway [LS151].

Why it unsettles: the ceiling stays an office ceiling while the ground under it changes nature and height. The same lid is far away in a hollow and touches the head on a crest. Height becomes a function of where you stand, not of the room.

**Move (Level 0, Office): one flat grid over a stepped floor.** Keep the building-standard 2 × 4 grid flat over a whole Standard room (2.9 m) and step the floor beneath it: a pit at −1.20 (A3), the datum (A1), a dais at +0.30 and +0.60 (A2), joined by B1 steps and one B7 ramp (R6: two ways onto each raised or sunken area). Headroom runs from 4.10 m in the pit to 2.30 m on the top dais, 0.25 m over the Relay's 2.05 m head. On the dais the hunter seems to fill the room. This is R3 pushed to its limit inside one room, and it is the space plan going wrong: the flat grid ignores the floor below it. Proposed as a named assembly, F8 (NEW, §5). In Level 0 it reads as a tiered training room fitted under an untouched ceiling; in Office as a trading floor on a raised floor next to a sunken bullpen.

### 1.2 *Blade Runner 2049* (2017). Production designer Dennis Gassner

Credits: cinematographer Roger Deakins; senior conceptual designer George Hull; conceptual design also by Sam Hudecki, Emmanuel Shiu, Ed Natividad, Victor James Martinez, Scott Lukowski and Peter Popken; Syd Mead as "visual futurist" [LS159].
- **Wallace's office:** "a large platform, sparsely furnished and surrounded by water, with ripples of light playing on the chamber's tall walls" [LS152]. Gassner took the idea from a Kyoto temple whose squeaking floors were an alarm: "We created an island and put water around it, because sound travels over water in a heightened fashion" [LS152].
- **Light that moves.** Deakins wanted "an artificial world in which lighting moves like sunlight": a ring of 256 Fresnels ran a slow chase round the platform, and eight 10K lamps lit the water so caustics spilled up the concrete walls. The pool floor was painted black and still reflected [LS152].

Why it unsettles: the island is a stage. The water isolates it and makes every approach visible and audible. The room is tall, bare concrete, and the only movement in it is light.

**Move (Pool): the audible island.** In a Tall hall (5.4 m, D5), a deck island of 2 × 3 cells (A7) stands off-centre in a basin (C1). Two ways on (R6): a 1.5 m bridge (A6) and a wading crossing on stepping stones (B8). The bridge deck rings and the water splashes, so sound carries both ways: the player hears the Relay cross, and it hears the player. Lit wet-niche lamps throw moving caustics on the tile walls (R8: the light moves but never leaves a dark walkable pocket). Era parts: a leisure pool's island deck, coping, a pool bridge, wet-niche lamps.

### 1.3 *Arrival* (2016). Production designer Patrice Vermette

- The shell hovers about 28–30 ft above the ground. The humans reach it by a scissor lift, "something very down to earth" against the alien technology [LS153].
- Inside, a gravity shift drops them at "the bottom end of a dark corridor". Its walls look like sedimentary rock: "the layers of the history and wisdom" of the visitors. At its end "a bright light to guide them" opens into a large calm chamber with one white screen, in a "James Turrell–like atmosphere" [LS153].

Why it unsettles: a long, dark, layered tube aimed at one bright, quiet room. You want the light, and the length makes you wait for it.

**Move (Run!): the strata corridor.** Keep the gravity out (no literal craziness, `20` §5). Take the strata: Run!'s soffit and walls carry generations of services, one layer per refit (old rigid conduit, then EMT, then a 1980s cable tray), so the corridor reads as the building's history in section. The layers thicken toward the goal: the lid steps down (D6, D1) to 2.45 m clear (10 RN-2), then releases into the bright, tall dock (T_RN_DockRelease, A2). Arrival's scissor lift becomes the dock's own humble machine, the pull-chain leveler at the threshold (`50` [LS60]).

### 1.4 *Dune* (2021) and *Dune: Part Two* (2024). Production designer Patrice Vermette

Senior concept artist George Hull on both films [LS159].
- **Arrakeen:** buildings angled against 550 mph winds, set in a mountain bowl, made of thick quarried blocks. "You would never have direct sun": "a system of light wells to distribute the light inside." The scale "diminishes human beings." Sets were built up to 24 ft high and continued in painted fabric above [LS154].
- **The imperial tent:** 42 ft tall, "a pyramid inside a pyramid", the court's hierarchy as a stepped section. Above it hangs a cube split into four by cross-shaped slits, which project a cross of light on the floor [LS154].

Why it unsettles: mass is the default and light is rationed through slots, so every patch of light marks a place. The tent's section ranks the people standing in it.

Symmetric: the tent is a centred pyramid. **Take** the rationed light and the stepped section. **Break it (R4):** the slot sits off the room's centre lines and the steps have unequal widths.

**Move (Pool): one slot of light.** In the pool hall, light comes only from the well (`20` §3), through one off-centre slot of 1 × 3 cells in the lid (D5). It falls across terraces of unequal width (A4: 0.60 m steps, 1, 2 and 1 cell deep) running down to the water (C1). The lit terrace is the weenie; the rest of the hall is lit evenly but lower (darkness is never cover).

### 1.5 *Andor* (2022). Production designer Luke Hull: Narkina 5

- "The prison not necessarily being a prison, but more like a labor camp", modelled on clean rooms and labs. "No bars"; very few guards; the floor itself is electrified, so barefoot prisoners are held by the ground [LS155].
- Hull designed "the entire building, and the function of it": shifts, where prisoners are cleansed, how they cross from the outer ring to the inner ring and the factory floor, "what's in the core", "even bits you will never see." Seven prisons, seven floors each, and every floor makes a different part: "an inverted panopticon" [LS155].
- The writers needed the plan to write the escape: "how long does that tunnel have to be?" [LS155].

Why it unsettles: the control is in the ground plane, not in walls. And because the whole process is designed, an escape can be worked out from the space.

**Move (all levels, method; Run! first): design the process before the corridor.** Run! is the back-of-house of a working building. Map its process first: where carts come from and go, which doors are fire doors, where the generator room and the transfer switch are, which way the egress runs. Then the trip to emergency power, the hunter's route and the player's loop all follow from a building that works. This is the legibility rule (`20` §5: mastery, not antagonism). Narkina's "every floor makes a different part" is our thesis in another fiction: one building, one layer per level.

### 1.6 *Squid Game* (2021; season 2, 2024). Production designer Chae Kyoung-sun

- The stair maze is a three-storey set where "no two hallways looked the same", built in sections that fit "almost like Lego bricks", by trial and error. Chae names Escher's *Relativity* (1953; `20` [LS38]) and an illustration of a child burning a ladder beside a tall wall: the stairs come from ladders [LS156].
- Her intent: "a staircase that repeats itself with no exit… a form of bondage." She wanted the maze corridor floating in the air; the set team refused it as unsafe. VFX added depth so the climb seemed endless [LS156].
- Season 2: the set is "about one and a half times bigger… built like a Lego module so it can be infinitely expanded, transformed, and rebuilt", with one more vertical layer [LS156].
- Its pink stairs are often compared with Bofill's La Muralla Roja (LS41, already read). Chae's own words in these sources name Escher, not Bofill: the link is the press's [UNVERIFIED as her source].

Why it unsettles: movement without progress. Every flight hides the next one, and the single-file climb never shows the whole.

**Move (Pool, deepest rooms only; one per floor at most, `20` §5 rule 2): the stair maze as F6 without spirals.** Blocks at independent levels (A2, A3 at ±0.60, ±1.20, ±1.80) joined only by straight flights (B2) and short bridges (A6), so the Relay can follow (it never uses B4). Each flight turns 90° off the last one, so no axis repeats (R4). Most routes come back to the level they started from; one goes down. The exit route stays legible from the top landing (E1 guards, R8 lit landings).

### 1.7 *Tenet* (2020). Production designer Nathan Crowley

- In the turnstile room, a glass wall divides one space into two halves: the forward side lit red, the inverted side blue. The Protagonist sees through the glass what happens in the other time [LS157]. Crowley: inversion "changes the whole way the set has to work" [LS157].

Symmetric: two mirrored halves. **Take** the idea that the state you will enter is visible through glass before you enter it. **Break it (R4):** the glass is never on the centre line.

**Move (Run!): the next stretch, seen tripped.** Each fire-door frame in the spine (every 9 m, 10 RN-2) has a wired-glass vision panel on its latch side, off-centre as real ones are (E3). Before the trip, the panel already shows the stretch beyond in emergency power: battery heads only. After the trip, the stretch behind you shows normal light. The corridor stays a corridor; only its doors show the other state.

### 1.8 *Parasite* (2019). Production designer Lee Ha-jun

- The kitchen stair, the basement store, the stair hidden behind a cabinet of jars, a narrow passage, a second flight and the bunker were all built as one vertical soundstage set, because the director wanted the descent filmed "in one breath" with a Steadicam [LS158].
- "The vertical structure of the house reflects the relations between the three families… Park's family mostly only go up the stairs, Kim and Geunse's families move up and down repeatedly." Bong called it a "staircase movie" [LS158].

Why it unsettles: the section is the story, and below the lowest floor you know about there is another one, reached from a storeroom.

**Move (Pool, the club's back room): the stair behind the shelving.** In the club's storage room (towels, chemicals, a 1990 back room), one steel shelving unit stands slightly off the wall. Behind it, a straight flight (B2, 12 risers, 1.80 m) leads to a 1-cell passage under a low lid (D1, 2.2 m), then a second flight down to the pool level. Two flights and a passage in one continuous run, never a spiral. It is not the only way down (R6), and it is not the EXIT (STAIR A stays the egress). It is the building lying: a floor under the lowest floor.

Not researched here: *Annihilation* (2018). Red listed it; no source was read, so no claim is made.

## 2. Games

Games add what film cannot: rules that must survive a player who turns round. Control's base game is LS29, Dishonored 2 LS37, Alien: Isolation LS30, The Exit 8 LS31.

### 2.1 *Control: AWE* (Remedy, 2020)

- The Investigations Sector was shut two years before the base game. Many rooms start in complete darkness and come back by restoring power. The player moves portable security lamps "to burn shadows away from doorways and thresholds". Rooms have a few strong sources: single glaring ceiling panels, floodlights, CRT glow [LS160].
- The geometry changes with the order: the base game's "rigid rectangles" and coffered ceilings give way to forms "far more aggressive and triangular" [LS160]. The hunter, Hartman, is immune in the dark, and "you never know when you're going to open a door" [LS160].
- From the base game's accessibility work: open areas were "deliberately designed with sightlines to see both open routes and locked doors", so players remember where to come back [LS160].

Why it unsettles: the threshold is the contested place, and light is a resource that structures the map. Conflict with our rules: AWE uses darkness as the hunter's domain. FrontRooms keeps darkness from ever being cover, for either side.

**Move (Run!, Level 0): lit thresholds and visible locked doors.** After the trip, battery heads sit at every fire-door frame and every junction (R8), so the spine reads as a chain of lit doors; the stretches between are dimmer but never dark enough to hide in (rule 12). In Level 0, the ring corridor keeps the locked Run! service doors in view from open areas: the player learns where they are long before they open (10 §0).

### 2.2 *Alan Wake 2* (Remedy, 2023): the Dark Place

GDC 2024, "The Dark Place of Alan Wake 2", by principal environment artist Nazareno Urbano; and a Level Design Summit talk on mission loops by lead level designer Anne-Marie Grönroos [LS161].
- **What the prototype taught.** "Flat city intersections are quite boring", especially at the game's slow walking speed. There was too much backtracking, and "the dark place was too dark": scale, proportion and silhouette needed more light to read [LS161].
- **The fix was section.** An elevated plaza as the centrepiece, an elevated walkway (the "Highline") beside it, a V-shaped intersection "instead of the classic cross", rooftop routes, and an alley set lower than the street. "Every key location of the game is visible from the center plaza" [LS161].
- **Impossible joins.** The same prefab placed twice, with a trigger halfway up a stair, moves the player without a visible cut. The Oceanview Hotel is small outside; past a skybridge the player is moved into a much larger underground layout [LS161].
- **Rewriting.** In the Writer's Room, plot elements swap "asset nodes", each holding every permutation of a scene [LS161].

Why it unsettles, and why it still plays: the space loops and lies, but every lie hangs off a legible skeleton (the plaza, the elevated line, the silhouettes).

**Moves.** (a) **Level 0: forks, not crosses.** Replace symmetric four-way crossings with three-way forks of unequal width, one branch a cell off the other's axis and one changing level by B1 steps or a B7 ramp (R4). (b) **Pool: the overlook deck.** One raised deck in the hall (A2 at +0.60, or an A5 gallery at +2.70), off-centre, from which every key place is in view: the EXIT route, the slide tower (F4), the mouth of the dark zone and the well's light. (c) **Pool: the lower route.** A channel (C4) one level below the deck path runs beside it, so the two routes see each other (R7).

### 2.3 *Cocoon* (Geometric Interactive, 2023). Designer Jeppe Carlsen

- Worlds inside orbs inside worlds; the player carries a world and dives into it [LS162].
- "You can only land in worlds at fixed points and we make sure to give you as much information in the single screen you land on without moving the camera at all… if there's a spot where you can leave an orb inside this world, frame it within that very first screen." Puzzles climb a "mental staircase" of small steps [LS162].

Why it matters: in a layered world, the arrival view is where the player's map is rebuilt. If that frame fails, they are lost.

**Move (every floor): the arrival frame.** STAIR A's door is the fixed landing point of every floor. The first view through it, with no turn, holds three things: the floor's one vertical event (the well's rail, a cut, a stepped room: E1, E3, D5), one lit route forward, and a marker you will recognise when you come back to the stair. This sharpens `20` §5 rule 5 (the far door can open onto a balcony over the new floor).

### 2.4 *Manifold Garden* (William Chyr, 2019)

- The world wraps in three dimensions: fall off an edge and you fall back into the same space, so the architecture repeats in every direction [LS163].
- Chyr's rules: avoid "vast areas of flatness"; use "subtle repeated architectural elements to guide the player"; "object height… no longer makes sense as reference points"; every element must have a purpose it can communicate ("intention") [LS163].

Why it unsettles: exact repetition seen in depth reads as infinite, Burke's "artificial infinite" (`20` [LS34]) turned vertical.

**Move (the well).** The stand-in shaft below each balcony repeats the structure exactly: slab edge, 1.07 m guard, column rhythm on the 6 m grid. That repetition carries the depth. The outlines and incidents vary per floor (`40` §7.5: a spiral in one corner, a bridge on one floor), so the player can still tell floors apart (R4). Unlike Manifold Garden, height stays a reference: floor numbers at every STAIR A door.

### 2.5 *Hitman* trilogy (IO Interactive, 2016–2021)

- Mette Andersen's GDC talk for IO Interactive sorts every space into six social types: public (squares, for breathing); public purpose (alleys); public rule (a church: strong known rules); private (trespass, but quiet breathers); private professional (kitchens: the space itself says who belongs); and private personal ("should be rare… it should feel special") [LS164].
- IO works with contrasts: Chongqing's rain-dark streets above a bright facility below; Sapienza's town over a lab in the caves; Dubai's new money against Dartmoor's old money [LS164].

Why it matters: a 1990 building already has this order. Front-of-house is public; back-of-house is "private professional"; the descent goes from one to the other.

**Move (Run!, Level 0, Office): the service world is always one door away.** Every public corridor has a back-of-house door at intervals (a service corridor, a janitor's closet, an electrical room). Its fittings tell you it is staff-only: VCT, cart bumpers, a closer, signage. Run! is the whole level made of that type, so it reads as "private professional" without a word (10 §0). Keep "private personal" rooms rare: one per level.

### 2.6 *Prey* (Arkane Austin, 2017): Talos I

- One continuous station. The GUTS is "like a highway that goes through a city… branches to each hub… you use this tunnel to deliver cargo or oxygen" [LS165]. The lobby "acts a bit like an interior facade" [LS165].
- Arkane's "level architects" turn the designers' greyboxes into rooms that make sense: "if somebody really worked here, what is their experience with this space." A level designer asks for "line of sight that… the exit is across the room, and it's well lit" [LS165].
- The elevator ambush happens in "a fake second elevator": during the power flicker the player is moved into a copy, and moved back when the fight ends [LS165].

**Moves.** (a) **Run! as the GUTS.** One service spine with short branches to each floor's service core and freight lift (10 §3: one spine with stubs). (b) **The exit rule, everywhere.** On entering any room, the way out is across the room, in sight and lit (R8, rule 12). (c) **The trip is the cut (Run!).** Run! already trips to emergency power when you enter. Like Arkane's flicker, the trip is the moment the corridor ahead can switch to its tripped variant (`20` V7, deep floors only). The corridor stays the same corridor in another state; its nature does not change.

Not used: *Lethal Company*, *Returnal*, *Viewfinder* and *Superliminal* were not researched in this pass. *Anemoiapolis* is a liminal-pool game of the same genre as Dream Pools; the composition guard applies, so it is left out.

## 3. Architecture

Built works give exact dimensions. Two precede 2010 (Seattle 2004, Vals 1996) and one is from 2007 (Ørestad): they were named in the brief or answer a rule of `40` directly.

### 3.1 Junya Ishigami, KAIT Workshop (Atsugi, 2008)

- One room of 1,989 m² (about 47 × 43 m) with 305 white steel columns and no walls or braces. Each column is a flat steel bar, from 16 × 145 mm to 63 × 90 mm, in "290 variations of a quadrilateral". 42 carry the roof; 263 are post-tensioned and resist earthquakes. They look random but are placed for the activities around them: "I was striving for ambiguity even among the columns" [LS166].
- Seen from one side, the wide faces line up into a wall; from another, the thin edges line up and the view opens [LS166].

Why it unsettles: there are no corridors, only densities, and the field's permeability depends on which way you look.

**Move (Office): the blade field (E5b, a variant of E5, §5).** A district of freestanding 1.52 m panels (the kit's panel width) on the 1.5 m sub-grid, single or in short L's, at the heights of 10 OF-1 (1.35, 1.65, 2.16 m). Two thirds face one axis, one third is turned 90°. Along the main axis you see edges and long open aisles; turn 90° and you face a wall of panel faces. No two neighbours repeat (R4). It is still an office: freestanding screens are the period's open-plan furniture [KNOWN, not re-read: the 1960s office landscape].

### 3.2 Junya Ishigami, KAIT Plaza (Atsugi, 2020)

- A column-free hall of about 80 × 50 m (longest span about 90 m) under one 12 mm steel plate that sags between the perimeter walls. The ceiling is only 2.2–2.8 m high. 59 unglazed openings, 1.8–3 m square, let in light and rain. The concave floor falls up to 5 m from its highest to its lowest point [LS167].
- Ishigami: "The sagging sky-like ceiling and the concave ground-like floor curve to join in the distance, thus materializing a horizon within the architecture. People appear as they approach from the horizon and disappear as they move beyond it." The low lid keeps daylight in pools under the openings; the rest is "moderately dimmer" [LS167].

Why it unsettles: a vast plan under a house-height lid. The lid itself becomes the horizon.

**Move (Level 0): the parallel bowl (F9, NEW, §5).** In a large open zone of the untenanted floor (6 × 8 cells, no partitions), the floor steps down two terraces of 0.60 m into a pit (A4, A3 at −1.20) and up again on the far side. The grid ceiling steps down with it (D6 at each terrace line), so the clear height stays 2.9 m everywhere. From one rim, the lowered lid over the pit cuts the view: a figure on the far rim shows only up to about 1.7 m, so the Relay's head and shoulders stay behind the soffit (computed for a 1-cell near terrace, a 3-cell pit and a 2-cell far terrace; check in greybox). The Relay is never in shadow; it is cut by the ceiling. Two ways in and out of the pit (R6); lamps clustered in pools, never leaving a dark pocket (R8).

### 3.3 SANAA, Rolex Learning Center (EPFL, Lausanne, 2010)

- One room of about 20,000 m² on a 166.5 × 121.5 m footprint. Floor and roof undulate "always in parallel". "Slopes instead of walls": hills, valleys and plateaus "often make the edges of the building invisible, though there are no visual barriers"; quiet zones are separated "through changes in height". 14 glazed patios are cut through it [LS168].
- The floor is carried by two concrete shells (camber up to 5 m) over a basement of parking, plant and archives. You enter from under the lifted edge into a patio. Three inclined lifts and five ramps replace stairs [LS168].

Why it unsettles: the floor hides and reveals; places exist without rooms.

**Move (Pool, Office): the crest that hides the water.** On the pool deck, a rise of 0.60 m (A2, reached by a B7 ramp on one side and B1 steps on the other, R6) hides the water beyond it. From the low deck you see the far wall and the bathers' heads, never the basin edge (C1 at −1.07). In Office, the same rule separates zones by height, not panels (`40` F3): from the sunken bullpen the edges of the floor vanish behind its rim.

### 3.4 OMA with LMN, Seattle Central Library (2004)

- The Books Spiral holds the whole nonfiction collection on levels 6 to 9 in one run: "aisles with a gradual slope of about 2 degrees connect the flat tiers of book stacks, creating a continuous ramp." The Dewey numbers run unbroken from 000 to the 900s, marked on floor mats. "A staircase at the center can be used as a shortcut" [LS169].
- The continuous-slope system was prototyped at full size, tested with disabled users and approved by the US Department of Justice in April 2001 [LS169].

Why it unsettles: you climb without a step. Only the numbers prove that you moved.

**Move (Office): the creeping slope (B9, NEW, §5).** A records district of lateral files on flat tiers, 2 cells deep, joined by aisles sloping about 1:30 (2°) over 3 cells (9 m), each rising 0.30 m. Two such aisles lift the floor 0.60 m without a single step. Under the flat grid (F8) the room shrinks from 2.9 to 2.3 m and nobody saw it happen. The drawer labels count up without a break. A straight flight (B2) cuts back down as the shortcut (R6). Never higher than +0.60, so the Relay keeps 0.25 m over its head.

### 3.5 MVRDV, Depot Boijmans Van Beuningen (Rotterdam, 2021)

- The first publicly accessible art store: 39.5 m high, 40 m across at the base and 60 m at the top. 14 storage compartments in five climate zones, sorted by material, not by period. 99 % of the building is public [LS170].
- The atrium runs from ground to roof: **40 m high, 28 m wide and only 6 m deep**. Five zigzag stairways cross it, "reminiscent of the etchings from Giovanni Piranesi", among 13 floating display cases. Windows into the storage compartments give "the impression of a panopticon". A guided group may enter a compartment for at most 11 minutes; then the climate needs 49 minutes to recover [LS170].

Why it unsettles: the back-of-house is the spectacle. The atrium is a slot, not a square, so the opposite wall and every crossing flight stay close.

The building is round; the atrium is not. **Take** the slot. **Moves:**
- **Office: the slot stair hall (F1b, a variant of F1, §5).** Instead of `20`'s square atrium, a slot 2 cells deep and 6–9 cells long (6 × 18–27 m) through two or three office floors. Straight flights (B2) cross it at different floors and in different directions, landings on alternating sides (R4). Both long walls are glazed into file rooms (E3). The Relay can use every flight.
- **Run!: storage behind mesh (E8, NEW, §5).** For two bays, the back-of-house corridor runs between tenant storage cages: chain-link from floor to slab. Sight and sound cross, bodies do not (R7): the Relay is visible through the cages two bays away. The cages are the corridor's walls, not new rooms, so Run! stays a corridor.
- **The well, as an option:** a slot of 2 × 5 cells instead of `20`'s 3 × 3 square cannot be ringed symmetrically. The gallery runs along one long side, the flights along the other.

### 3.6 Heatherwick Studio, Vessel (New York, 2019)

- 154 interconnected flights, about 2,500 steps, 80 landings, 16 storeys, 150 ft high. The studio cites the stepwells of Rajasthan and "the mesmerizing visual effect of the repeating steps, flights and landings" [LS171].
- It closed in 2021 after four people died by suicide in 18 months. It reopened on 21 October 2024 with floor-to-ceiling steel mesh on four stairwells and their platforms, about half the former open area; the top level stays closed. The mesh "had to be designed differently on each level" [LS171].

Why it unsettles: a lattice of choices where every climber sees the others across the void. It also shows that the original guards on a climbable void were not enough: the fix was floor-to-ceiling mesh.

Symmetric: radial. **Take** the lattice of short flights and the stepwell's descent. **Break it (R4):** one sector against one wall, never a ring.

**Move (Pool): the stepwell wall.** On one side of the pool hall only, a lattice of short straight flights (B2, 4–6 risers) and 1-cell landings (A2, A5) zigzags down from the club level to the deck, in a 3 × 6-cell sector. Its period part is the natatorium bleacher (B6). Where the drop exceeds one storey, the open face is mesh (E8), as at Vessel since 2024; elsewhere E1 guards. The other three sides of the hall stay flat.

### 3.7 Peter Zumthor, Therme Vals (Graubünden, 1996)

- Set into the hillside: fifteen table-like blocks, each 5 m high, 3–5 m wide and 6–8 m long, concrete cores clad in local gneiss. Each block carries its own roof slab; the slabs do not touch, and the 8 cm glazed gaps between them bring in light. 16 small blue rooflights sit over the central pool [LS172].
- Inside the blocks are small rooms entered at right angles: a fire bath at 42 °C, a cold plunge (12 °C in one source, 10 °C in another), a flower bath at 30 °C, a drinking well, and a completely dark "sounding stone". "There is no predetermined sequence": visitors make their own path [LS172].
- The way in: from the hotel through a dark underground passage, past spring-water taps and the changing rooms, onto a raised platform with the pool in view, then down "a long processional ramp" [LS172].

Why it unsettles: solid and void swap roles. The rooms are inside the solids; the pool is the space left between them; light enters only through fissures.

**Move (Pool, the club): wet blocks (C9, NEW, §5).** Tiled blocks of 1 × 2 to 2 × 3 cells, 5.0 m tall in the Tall hall (5.4 m), stand in and around the pool. Each holds one room of a 1990 club (whirlpool, steam room, cold plunge, sauna), entered at a right angle. The blocks pinwheel; no two share an axis (R4). A linear lamp slot in the lid along one edge of each block stands in for Vals's fissures. The route between them is free. No block is dark (rule 12): the "sounding stone" is the one thing not taken. The way in follows Vals in our module: from the lockers onto a platform at +0.90 (A2) with the hall in view, then a B7 ramp in three runs down to the deck.

### 3.8 Zaha Hadid Architects, London Aquatics Centre (2012)

- An 11,000 m² roof spans a column-free hall 160 m long and up to 90 m wide, on two concrete cores and a 22 m wall. Its form "is generated by the sightlines for the spectators". "The lower surface dips between the diving and competition pools": the roof alone separates the two volumes [LS173].
- The diving pool is 25 × 21 × 5 m with platforms at 1, 3, 5, 7.5 and 10 m, in sculpted concrete. The roof is singly symmetric [LS173].

Why it matters: one hall split into two rooms by the lid alone, and a tower of offset platforms as the vertical event.

Symmetric on one axis (R4 allows it); offset it anyway. **Move (Pool): the lid divides.** In a Tall hall (5.4 m), the lid drops to a 2.9 m soffit band (D6) between the lap basin and the diving well, at a third of the hall's length, not the middle: two volumes, no wall. A 1990 diving tower with platforms at 1, 3 and 5 m, each set back as it rises, stands over the 4–5 m diving well (`20` §4.4: depth seen, not entered). Its stair is straight (B2, B3), never a spiral. The 3 m platform is the hall's overlook (§2.2).

### 3.9 Maggie's Centres; OMA, Maggie's Gartnavel (Glasgow, 2011)

- **The brief** (Maggie's, 2015) says hospitals make patients feel like "very small cogs in a very large machine", and lists what demoralises: "the closed doors implying secrets withheld, the endless corridors, the signposting, the artificial light". Maggie's scale is domestic, "the antithesis of the hospital's"; "we don't have signs, even on the toilets". Rooms should offer "corners to tuck up in with a book, but also places where they can sit and watch, but not necessarily join in" [LS174].
- **Gartnavel:** one storey, a ring of interlocking L-shaped rooms round a planted court, "seemingly haphazardly arranged", with almost no corridors. Rooms step down the slope by gentle ramps, "creating different levels of intimacy through subtly changing ceiling heights", under one 350 mm concrete roof slab, "a continuous datum" that cantilevers up to 8 m [LS174].

Why it matters: the brief is an exact list of the institutional uncanny. Gartnavel adds a flat datum over a stepped floor (F8) used for comfort.

A ring (Red's note): **take** the datum and the interlocking L's, not the ring. **Moves.** (a) The four demoralisers are already the tools of Level 0 and Run! (10 §2.2): use them knowingly, and never in the safe zones. (b) The stairwell landing and the station follow the brief's inverse: a corner to tuck into, a place to sit and watch the floor through glass (E3), steady light, no new signs beyond the code's EXIT.

### 3.10 Sou Fujimoto, Musashino Art University Library (Tokyo, 2010) and House NA (Tokyo, 2010)

- **The library** is made of shelving only: "a place encircled by a single bookshelf in the form of a spiral", "9 m high walls punctuated by large apertures". Circulation winds between apertures cut out of the shelves; the radial apertures give "an infinite depth of books"; the shelf becomes the outer wall [LS175].
- **House NA:** 21 floor plates at different heights, 21 to 81 sq ft each, joined by stairs, ladders and fixed and movable steps. The steps "become seating and desks… a device segmenting a territory" [LS175].

Why it matters: the furniture is the architecture, and a line of aligned openings turns layers of stuff into depth.

**Moves.** (a) **Office: one line through the stuff.** In a records district, 2.16 m shelving and lateral files stand in parallel layers, 1 cell apart. Most openings are staggered; one line of openings aligns through four layers to a lit point (the weenie). One long view cuts through the stuff, and the Relay can be seen along it (R7). Not a full spiral (R4, and Red's note on rings). (b) **Pool club: the viewing lounge as plates.** 1-cell plates step down 0.30 m at a time toward the glass over the pool (A2, E3); the steps double as seats (B6): refuge with a view.

### 3.11 Herzog & de Meuron, Elbphilharmonie (Hamburg, 2016–17)

- The "Tube" is an 82 m escalator, "slightly curved, so that the end of the escalator is not visible from the beginning". It gives "the feeling of diving into an atmospheric tunnel", lined with about 8,000 sequins, a ride of about 2.5 minutes. It ends at a panorama window; a second 20 m escalator rises to the Plaza at 37 m [LS176].

Why it unsettles: the path's own curve hides its end, and the reward is a view.

**Move (Pool, the club): the curved passage.** The passage from the lockers to the pool hall curves on an inscribed 3.0 m radius (E4, R5) under a low lid (D1, 2.4 m). Its outer wall is glass block, a 1980s club material (18 PS), so the pool's light glows through it before the end comes into view. It ends at a window onto the hall (E3), then the Vals platform and ramp (§3.7).

### 3.12 3XN, Ørestad College (Copenhagen, 2007)

- Four boomerang-shaped floor plates are rotated round a tall central atrium. "The rotation opens a part of each floor to the central atrium"; each floor is level within itself. The stair is 4 m wide, turns a quarter turn per floor and is "much wider than mere transportation needs… it becomes a space in itself" [LS177].

Why it matters: one floor plate, rotated per floor, makes a void whose outline differs on every floor. Stacked, never repeated.

**Move (the well).** This is the built precedent for `40` §7.5. One outline of cells (an L, not a square) is turned 90° per floor, so the void's edge differs on every floor while every floor plate is the same part. The crossings are straight flights; large stairs belong to the parallel session (`60`).

Not researched in this pass: BIG, Lacaton & Vassal, Toyo Ito's Tama Art University Library, Termas Geométricas. No claim is made about them.
