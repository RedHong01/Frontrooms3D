# R3 · Swiss style: how type and pictograms speak one language

Research for the FrontRooms iconography system (平面视觉). Started 2026-10-30.
Question: in the International Typographic Style and the wayfinding/pictogram tradition that grew out of it,
which concrete rules (stroke, grid, geometry, proportion, alignment, spacing, colour, level of reduction)
keep type and symbols consistent, and how were pictogram systems designed together with their typefaces?

FrontRooms context: 1990 building-services print language (ISO 23601 evacuation plans, ISO 7010 / ANSI Z535 signs,
office forms, CRT screens). Faces: TeX Gyre Heros (Helvetica clone; Bold / Condensed Bold) for signage and print,
Bayon (HUD display caps), IBM Plex Mono (meta, digits), Source Serif 4 (captions, body), Courier Prime (key numerals).

Conventions in this file
- Every fact carries a source id in [S##] that matches a row in R3_sources.tsv (M01 = my own local measurement).
- "read" = I read the page (WebFetch); "snippet" = known only from a search-result snippet.
- INFERENCE = my own reasoning, not stated by the source.
- Source wording is paraphrased (copyright); page or section numbers are given wherever known so the original wording can be checked. One short quote is kept (Neurath, §3).
- No images or PDFs were downloaded; reference images are listed as page URL + image URL only.

## 1. Otl Aicher · Munich 1972 (and Lufthansa, ZDF, ERCO, Rotis)

### Facts
- Aicher was lead designer of the Munich 1972 Games and built the pictograms with a series of grid systems and a dedicated bright palette [S09]. Univers was the only face used in official Games communication (Rathgeb, as cited on Fonts In Use) [S02]; Aicher's criterion was that the face be modern, and he saw serif faces as old-fashioned [S02].
- Grid: square grids, with every element of a figure at 45° or 90° [S03]. Ellen Lupton (Cooper Hewitt) compares the system to designing a typeface whose glyphs are body parts, and says Aicher wanted it to work like an alphabet [S06]. Cooper Hewitt: rules and grids fixed the proportions of heads, torsos and features [S05].
- Modular body: each figure splits into upper and lower body, with the same body parts and the same gap between parts; flat solid black on white, no faces, no depth [S04].
- Scale of the set: the 1972 poster shows 166 pictograms (169 squares on silver-grey), most of them practical information (stairs, toilets, transit), not sport [S05][S06]. The same principles were applied to sport symbols and to toilet/stair/train signs [S06].
- Process: Gerhard Joksch sketched loosely on tracing paper, artworkers redrew them crisply; nobody had time to check every pictogram against the grid [S01].
- The grid myth: Mark Holt (2025) shows that a limb made of one orthogonal and one 45° part cannot keep a single thickness on Aicher's grid; his finer 45° grid makes 45° limbs match orthogonal ones, makes all gaps uniform and puts heads in consistent positions [S01]. An FH Potsdam analysis (2017) independently found that the pictograms do not sit on one template grid and vary in proportion and in the gaps to external items (e.g. the fencing sword) [S04].
- Colour: light blue and white dominate, with green, orange, yellow and silver [S03][S06]; Eye lists yellow, orange, fresh green, pale and dark blue [S07]. Red and black, tied to Berlin 1936 and to political power, were kept out [S03]. The manual gives colour a regulating and recognition role (section 4.1) and lays out all print areas in the Didot system (section 8.1); its artwork is dated June 1969 [S02].
- Medium-specific terminals: for the ZDF logo (1973) testing showed the sharp ends of Univers blurred on screen, so Aicher redrew them with round terminals [S07].
- Lufthansa (Ulm HfG Entwicklungsgruppe 5, study 1962, manual 1963): crane reduced to basic forms, put in a circle, made exact with a grid; Helvetica as house face for 50+ years [S08][S03].
- ERCO: pictograms dated 1976 (an extension of the Munich sign work); ERCO pictogram luminaires carry Aicher's safety signs; a Frankfurt airport sign system was done with ERCO lighting [S03][S10]. Rotis (Agfa, 1988), named after his studio; ERCO and Bulthaup later used it [S09][S10].

### What this means for type + icon consistency
- The Munich lesson is one alphabet of parts: a small kit (head disc, torso, limb bar) placed only at 0/45/90° on a square module, with one colour code and one sans (Univers) [S03][S05][S06].
- INFERENCE: Holt's critique is a concrete rule for us: when a stroke turns from 90° to 45°, its perpendicular thickness must stay the same; draw diagonals as offset paths of constant width, not as grid cells, or the 45° limbs read thinner/heavier than the verticals [S01].
- INFERENCE: the ZDF fix shows that terminals should match the output medium: on a blurry CRT, rounded or slightly heavier ends hold up better than sharp ones; in print, square ends match Heros.

## 2. Lance Wyman · Mexico 68 and the Mexico City Metro (1969)

### Facts
- Logotype: the five Olympic rings were developed into the numerals 68 and joined to MEXICO by radiating parallel lines; on the poster the lines run out to all four borders [S11]. Wyman did this by adjusting line widths and counter spacing of the ring geometry [S12]. The lettering is built from three parallel stripes (99PI narration) [S13].
- One line language for everything: Wyman says he developed the typography for the word Mexico out of that logo [S13]; Bierut (2004) described the concentric stripes growing into a custom alphabet [S12], used to name the sports venues legibly at long distance [S12]. The stripes went onto uniforms, hats, stamps and balloons [S13].
- Pictograms: not stick figures; Wyman says each focused on a body part, a piece of equipment, or both (gymnastics = a hand gripping a ring) [S13]. A rationale set before Wyman joined asked for one recognisable gesture or equipment detail per sport [S12]. Three symbol sets were coordinated: sports, cultural programme (19 + 19 icons), regulatory messages [S12][S14].
- Frame and form: bold simple forms in square frames with radiused corners [S12]; Wyman's later icons keep the same simple shapes and rounded corners [S14].
- Colour as code: each sport had its colour, so a visitor could simply follow, for example, the green signs to the football stadium (99PI) [S13]; tickets colour-coded by day carried brand, stadium, sport icon, date, time and price [S12]; Huichol artisans joined the colour studies [S12].
- Signage hardware (Peter Murdoch): modular poles + frames; icon panels with minimal text grouped on pylons [S12].
- Metro 1969: Wyman derived a custom typeface from the geometry of the Metro M logo and called it architectural [S14]; each line colour-coded, a unique icon per station [S13]. Station icons came from a historical image, an existing structure, the station name or a local function (Chapultepec = grasshopper hill → grasshopper) [S13][S14]. A Metro sign holds three units: the M, the station icon, a directional arrow [S14]. Wyman's aim: a hierarchy of icons instead of a mass of words [S14].

### What this means for type + icon consistency
- The strongest model of a shared language: one geometric generator (ring/stripe) produces logotype, alphabet, pattern and frames, so type and image share stroke rhythm and corner treatment [S11][S12][S13].
- INFERENCE: for FrontRooms the generator should be the 1990 sign hardware itself (Heros stems, ISO safety shapes, 45° arrow), not a new motif; Wyman shows that a shared container (rounded-corner square) plus a shared colour code holds a heterogeneous icon set together even when each icon picks its own subject.
- INFERENCE: the Metro three-unit sign (logo + station icon + arrow) is a direct template for an EXIT/room plate: fixed slots, fixed order, icon carries the meaning, text is secondary.

## 3. Isotype · Otto Neurath, Marie Neurath (Reidemeister), Gerd Arntz (1925–1971)

### Facts (Neurath's own rules, International Picture Language, 1936 [S15]; page numbers for checking)
- Signs behave like letters: they must be "so simple that they may be put in lines like letters" (pp. 32–33).
- One language: all pictures in a chart form one unit in one language and are combined by the same rules (pp. 28–29); signs must also differ enough that their names are never in doubt (p. 32).
- Reduction: fact pictures carry the least possible detail (p. 64) and are the opposite of a naturalistic or camera picture (p. 64).
- Quantity by repetition: more signs stand for more things; making signs bigger or smaller is unclear (pp. 73–74). Kinross summarises the rule as repeating the unit instead of enlarging it [S16]; the Arntz archive says quantity is never shown by symbol size [S17].
- Colour: Isotype uses only seven colours (p. 42); colour carries category, e.g. farming green, industry red, business and transport blue, other groups grey (p. 48); colours must differ enough to leave no doubt (p. 44); pictures must still work in black and white, using shades of black, lines or dots (p. 46).
- Reading order: like a book, top left to bottom right (p. 79); units sit side by side (p. 80).
- Words + pictures: Neurath's slogan casts words as dividing and pictures as connecting (p. 18), yet the picture language needs ordinary words to tell its story (p. 16).

### Facts (Arntz, symbols and type)
- Arntz gave Isotype its look; he chose woodcut, later linocut, for its primitive strength and reproducibility [S18]. More than 4,000 pictograms; every size needed its own hand-cut linocut [S17].
- Symbols were modules: symmetrical, with weight distributed so they sit well in rows; they were made as parts of whole charts, not as standalone images [S19].
- Compound signs: a generic sign (man) gets a specific meaning from a standard modifier (cap, industry sign), e.g. a steel-industry worker [S17].
- Type: the Vienna museum adopted Futura for all its printed matter around 1929, for titles, captions and numbers; the choice predates Arntz [S20][S19]. Christopher Burke's chapter on Arntz in Futura: the typeface (2016/2017) links Futura and Arntz's pictograms [S21].
- The transformer (Marie Neurath) specified title, arrangement, type, number and colour of symbols, a blueprint the artist then executed (Kinross) [S19][S18].

### What this means for type + icon consistency
- Isotype is the origin of the idea that a pictogram is a glyph: same size, same weight, set in lines, read like text, governed by one rule set [S15].
- INFERENCE: one linocut per size = optical sizing for icons; FrontRooms icons at HUD size, plate size and print size should be separate drawings (thicker strokes, fewer details, wider gaps at small sizes), not one vector scaled.
- INFERENCE: Isotype pairs a geometric sans (Futura) with flat silhouettes of even colour; the sans carries words and numbers, the sign carries the thing. For FrontRooms, Heros/Bayon carry words, Plex Mono carries numbers, and icons never carry text inside them.
- INFERENCE: compound signs (base + modifier) are the cleanest way to grow a set: a base DOOR glyph + modifiers (locked, exit, fire) instead of separate drawings.

## 4. AIGA / US DOT Symbol Signs · Cook & Shanosky (1974, +16 in 1979)

### Facts (primary source: the 1974 report, DOT-OS-40192, 228 pp., read via archive.org OCR [S22])
- Committee (AIGA Signs and Symbols Committee): Thomas H. Geismar (chair), Seymour Chwast, Rudolph de Harak, John Lees, Massimo Vignelli; coordinators Don and Karen Moyer; drawing by Cook and Shanosky Associates; guidelines by Page, Arbitrio and Resen [S22].
- Method: 34 messages in 4 groups (public services, concessions, processing activities, regulations); existing symbols from 24 sources (Tokyo 64, Mexico 68, Munich 72, Expo 67/70, airports, Dutch Railways …) sorted into concept groups; each scored 1–5 by every committee member on three dimensions (report section 3) [S22][S23]:
  - semantic: how well the image represents the message;
  - syntactic: how the image relates to the other images (consistency, hierarchy of recognition);
  - pragmatic: whether a person can actually see it (visibility, lighting, vandalism, reproduction, enlargement and reduction) [S22].
- Drawing rules (section 4): simplification is named as what makes the set coherent; all drawings share one optically consistent line weight for unity; edges softened with curves (glass base, limbs); dark figure on light ground; symmetrical about a vertical axis where possible; each symbol in a square field with radiused corners [S22].
- Type pairing (section 5): no face is mandated, but the guideline illustrations use Helvetica Medium (caps and lower case), chosen for legibility, compatibility with the symbols and aesthetic quality; its large x-height lets mixed case work [S22].
- Arrow pairing: the arrow style was chosen for compatibility with Helvetica Medium [S22]. The arrow sits on the side it points to; with several directions it keeps a constant position relative to the symbols [S22].
- Size ratio type : symbol: the illustrations use letter-height-to-symbol ratios of 1/4, 1/3 and 2/5; symbols may be larger but should not be less legible than the type [S22].
- Spacing grid (from the OCR of the guidelines): one symbol width between lettering and symbol, half a symbol width between arrow and symbol, a quarter symbol width between symbols [S22]. (I could not see the illustration; check it before adopting the first figure.)
- Distance: 1 inch of letter height per 50 feet of viewing distance [S22]; a 3-inch symbol reads at ~30 ft, a 12-inch one at ~155 ft; keep signs within ~10° of the line of sight [S23].
- Colour: black figure on white field, never reversed; red (Ostwald, today Pantone 032 C) only for First Aid / No Smoking / No Parking / No Entry; 1979 adds Pantone 340 C green for Exit; prohibitions use the international red circle with diagonal bar; white lettering on a dark ground needs more letterspacing than black on white; translations in the same lettering style but another colour [S22][S23].
- Process: almost a year for the first 34, hand-painted on hundreds of tracing sheets, committee notes filled a binder; the male figure was nicknamed Helvetica Man after Cook's favourite face; the walking figure was left off the stairs symbol as too busy but kept on the escalator [S24].
- Status: US-government work, public domain [S23].
- Critique: Ministry of Type finds the shops symbol overly dense and warns that Exit and No Entry may be confused if signs are not hung level; consistency must not erase the difference between messages [S25].

### What this means for type + icon consistency
- This is the clearest written proof that the classic sign system matched symbols, arrows and type deliberately: one optical line weight across all symbols, an arrow chosen to match Helvetica Medium, and a numeric type-to-symbol size ratio (letter height = 1/4 to 2/5 of the symbol field) [S22].
- INFERENCE: for FrontRooms plates set in Heros Bold / Condensed Bold, draw the arrow from the Heros stem (shaft = stem width) and fix letter height at about 1/3 of the icon field as the default ratio.
- INFERENCE: the no-reversal rule and the extra-letterspacing rule for white on dark both apply to our ANSI/ISO panels: reversed (white on red/green) text needs added tracking, and pictograms keep their figure/ground polarity.

## 5. Adrian Frutiger · Univers (1957), Roissy/Frutiger (1970–76), Signs and Symbols (1978/1989)

### Facts
- Univers as a system: released 1957 (Deberny & Peignot); two-digit names, first digit = weight, second = width/posture; in the Linotype version fonts of one weight share cap height, x-height, ascender and descender, and widths change consistently with weight; one of the first families built as a matched set so one face could serve a whole document [S29]. Frutiger rejected purely geometric type as too rigid and aimed for a sensitive modulation between thick and thin [S29].
- Roissy (Charles de Gaulle airport): commissioned 1970/71, completed 1972, developed from his Concorde face; Linotype released it as Frutiger in 1976. Goals: legibility at different angles, sizes and distances; prominent ascenders/descenders, wide apertures [S26]. Frutiger found Univers too round and closed for fast recognition of sign words [S27].
- Signs at CDG: banana-yellow background (chosen with a colour specialist), black lettering, French above / English below, a bolder expanded cut for Départ, short stubby arrows for direction [S27]. Frutiger's later self-critique: the Roissy face was too light and set too tightly; the signs needed more space around the letters [S27].
- Pictograms at CDG: the Ladies/Gentlemen signs were not always understood; pictograms were brought in because many travellers do not read the Latin alphabet, and Frutiger argues a language of signs is easier to learn than written word images [S28]. Not everything can become a pictogram (e.g. an airport lounge) [S27].
- Principles: Frutiger wanted total clarity, free of any artistic addition [S26]; type should be felt by the reader rather than noticed, and legibility and beauty sit close together [S28].
- Signs and Symbols (German 1978 Der Mensch und seine Zeichen; EN 1989, tr. Andrew Bluhm): starts from the six faces of a die; asks why certain primary shapes and configurations of dot and line are perceived and remembered more easily than others; 2,000+ line drawings; treats pictograms, alphabets, numerals, symbols and trademarks as one continuum [S30]. A Visible Language article citing Frutiger notes that most so-called pictograms (e.g. the toilet sign) are really ideograms with a conventional meaning [S30]. (Full text not publicly readable; only secondary summaries used.)

### What this means for type + icon consistency
- Frutiger treats letters and pictograms as one family of signs built from dot and line; the design question for both is the same: which configurations are perceived fastest [S30].
- Univers shows the typographic version of an icon keyline: a family where every weight keeps the same vertical metrics, so different weights sit on one baseline and one cap line [S29]. INFERENCE: an icon family should do the same: one fixed live area + cap-line relation, several weights.
- Frutiger's own correction (Roissy too light, too tight) is a direct rule for signs: sign type and sign symbols need more weight and more space than text settings [S27]. INFERENCE: Heros Bold (not Regular) at loose tracking on plates; icons drawn at the bold weight with open gaps.
- INFERENCE: his toilet-sign remark matters for the game's tone: many pictograms are conventions the player must already know (ISO/ANSI), so FrontRooms should reuse conventional ideograms where they exist and invent new ones only for game-only concepts.

## 6. Massimo Vignelli / Bob Noorda (Unimark) · NYCTA Graphics Standards Manual (1966–70)

### Facts
- Unimark (Vignelli, Noorda) hired 1966/67; manual issued 1970 after ~4 years, built partly on Noorda's mid-1966 traffic-flow research [S31][S32]. Museum summaries: one sans with a limited palette of bright colours plus black and white [S34].
- Type: specified as Standard Medium (Akzidenz-Grotesk), later Helvetica [S34][S31]; Vignelli first used Helvetica on the 1972 map; Helvetica Medium became the official sign face only in December 1989 (Michael Hertz Associates update) [S32]. Paul Shaw's essay/book (AIGA 2008; MIT Press 2011) is the standard account of the Standard → Helvetica story [S35].
- The grid is set by the type: the manual's page index lists a grid page keyed to an upper-case X height of 1 3/8 in (p. 8), letter-spacing pages (pp. 9–10), and Standard Medium alphabets at 9 in (p. 88) and 1 3/8 in (pp. 168–169) [S33]. INFERENCE: the layout unit of every sign = the cap height of the type at that size.
- Spacing: the manual describes a modular unit system that gives consistent letter and word spacing for its three type sizes and demands strict adherence (as quoted via Slate) [S36]; it also fixes word spacing, leading and number of lines per sign [S32].
- Modules: all signs are built from 1'×1' panels: 1'×2' information, 1'×4' direction, 1'×8' station identification [S32]; a page on sign plate modulation (p. 62) [S33].
- Arrow: a page on the construction of the arrow (p. 55) [S33]; Jesse Reed points to a four-degree reduction of the diagonal arrow's bar, an optical rather than mechanical correction [S36]. Arrows are built into the type-bearing signboards [S31].
- Colour code: route symbols are coloured discs; index pages on 5½ in colour-coding discs (p. 40), smaller discs (p. 45) and colour swatches (p. 46) [S33].
- The black band: the black stripe at the top marked the metal channel that hung the signs (stations could not be altered structurally); the manual then made the band part of the graphics wherever a panel needed another structure [S32]. Signs went from black-on-white to white-on-black in 1973 against graffiti; a thin white line survives as a trace of the stripe [S32].

### What this means for type + icon consistency
- The NYCTA manual derives everything (grid, spacing, sign module, disc size) from one measure, the cap height of the face, and fixes letter/word spacing as units; symbols (discs, arrows) are sized in the same units [S33][S36].
- Its arrow is a drawn glyph with optical correction (4° on the diagonal), not a pure geometric construction: icons next to type need the same optical overshoot/thinning tricks type uses [S36].
- INFERENCE: the black band is a precedent for FrontRooms: a hardware trace (mounting channel) turned into a graphic constant. Our 1990 plates can carry a similar band (e.g. a top rule in Heros-stem thickness) that ties icon plates and text plates into one family.

## 7. Grids and programmes · Müller-Brockmann, Ruder, Gerstner, Hofmann, Crouwel (+ Wissing at Schiphol)

### Facts
- Müller-Brockmann, Grid Systems in Graphic Design (Niggli 1981): grid fields match a number of text lines in depth and the column in width; gaps between fields are one, two or more text lines vertically and depend on type and illustration size horizontally; illustrations, photographs and statistics span one to four fields; the smallest illustration = the smallest field; fewer size variants give a calmer design; typography, photography, illustration and colour all fit the fields [S37]. He presents the grid as the expression of a mental attitude: constructive and future-oriented [S37].
- Ruder, Typographie (Niggli 1967, 274 pp., 19 chapters incl. Form and counter-form, Lettering and illustration): he names two ways to harmonise type and picture: the closest possible formal match, or a deliberate contrast [S38]. Letter combinations depend on the interplay of the white inside the letters (counters) and the white of the set width; printed and unprinted areas must stand in tension; a book should be designed consistently throughout, title page and cover included [S38]. A dealer's commentary notes Ruder's nine-square grid as the basis for different image sizes [S38].
- Gerstner, Designing Programmes (1964; German Programme entwerfen 1963): summaries give his maxim as programmes for solutions instead of solutions for problems [S39]; he calls the grid, understood as a proportional regulator, a programme par excellence (1967, p. 16); the designer builds structures from which single solutions are derived (p. 74); typography is an art precisely because it serves a purpose (p. 58) [S40]. The Capital magazine mobile grid (1962–63) has 58 modules superimposing five grids (1–6 columns) [S41]; basic unit 10 pt (search snippet only) [S39].
- Hofmann, Graphic Design Manual (1965): sections The Dot, The Line, Confrontation (dissimilar elements such as form and lettering), Letters and Signs; a step-by-step course from elementary elements to complex work [S42].
- Crouwel, New Alphabet (1967): for CRT displays and phototypesetting; only horizontal and vertical strokes, with 45° corners as the one diagonal; most letters on a 5 × 7 unit grid [S43]; Reynolds notes that low-resolution CRT screens could not render curves well [S44]. Crouwel later called it excessive, never meant for real use and unreadable [S43].
- Schiphol (Benno Wissing / Total Design, 1967): text for destinations, arrows for direction, yellow for arrivals/departures, green for everything else (Rijksmuseum, which holds a sign box) [S45]; Mijksenaar's 1990s update kept the codes and made gate numbers, arrows, pictograms and Frutiger type consistent across all signs; the Wim Crouwel Instituut traces this to the Isotype legacy [S45][S46].

### What this means for type + icon consistency
- The Swiss grid is derived from the type (line height, column), and every non-text element is sized to grid fields; icons are therefore sized in text-line units, not free sizes [S37].
- Ruder names the two legitimate strategies: icon and type as closest formal match, or as deliberate contrast. FrontRooms needs both: icons that match Heros (signage) and, where wanted, a deliberate contrast in the HUD (Bayon caps + icon) [S38].
- Gerstner's programme idea = write the icon rules so new icons can be derived without redesign (a parametric programme, not a set of drawings) [S40].
- INFERENCE: Crouwel's New Alphabet is the closest historical model for CRT icons: on a raster, use only 0/90° strokes with 45° chamfers on a unit grid (5 × 7 is the classic dot-matrix cell). That gives the in-game CRT screens a period-correct icon dialect that still shares the 0/45/90° angle system with the print icons.
- INFERENCE: Schiphol shows a minimal colour code (yellow = primary flow, green = everything else) carried by panels, while pictograms stay one colour; FrontRooms can likewise give colour to the plate, not to the icon.

## 8. Jock Kinneir & Margaret Calvert · UK road signs (Anderson 1957–58, Worboys 1963–65), Transport typeface

### Facts
- Brief: motorway signs from 1957 (after their Gatwick airport scheme), then all other British road signs from 1963 (Worboys Committee), including symbols and pictograms to replace word-heavy signs [S47][S48]. Kinneir's principles: clarity, a system that would not date, and a minimal set of guidelines for standardisation [S48]. Kinneir compared consistency in design to grammar in language [S49].
- Transport lettering: strongly influenced by Akzidenz-Grotesk (Calvert), with Johnston's curved-tail l; mixed case for word shape at speed [S48]. Calvert calls it lettering rather than typography: it had to work black on white, white on black and white on colour [S49]. Two weights of one form: Transport Heavy for black on white, Transport Medium (lighter) for white on dark grounds such as green primary-route signs [S50].
- Shape classes: triangles warn, circles instruct, rectangles inform (European protocol) [S49]. Colour: motorways white on blue (blue recedes, so the eye goes to the white names, per Calvert), primary routes white on green with yellow route numbers, local routes black on white [S48].
- Pictograms: mostly drawn by Calvert; children crossing based on Calvert as a child; the cattle sign on a cow called Patience; men at work, which she jokingly reads as a man struggling with an umbrella [S49]. Pictograms follow those recommended by the 1949 UN conference [S50].
- One measuring unit for type and symbol: UK working drawings give non-text dimensions in stroke widths (sw), with 4 sw = x-height; one drawing sets all symbols at 14 sw high (to tile outlines); text in Transport Heavy at given x-heights [S51]. A derived manual (Transport Malta) gives the same stroke width = 1/4 x-height, tile height = 2 x-heights, word space 2.5 sw, standard border 1.5 sw [S51].

### What this means for type + icon consistency
- The UK system is the most explicit same-unit rule found: the letter stroke (1/4 x-height) is the unit for spacing, borders, symbol height and layout, so letters, borders and pictograms belong to one weight family [S51].
- Weight changes with polarity: Medium for light-on-dark, Heavy for dark-on-light (irradiation compensation) [S50]. INFERENCE: FrontRooms icons need a reversed-polarity variant about one step lighter (white icon on red/green/black panel) and a positive variant at full weight, mirroring Transport Medium/Heavy and the DOT rule that reversed lettering needs more spacing.
- INFERENCE: for Heros signage, measure the Heros stem at the chosen x-height (or cap height) and express border, gap, arrow shaft and icon stroke in multiples of that stem, the way UK drawings use sw.

## 9. Standards · ISO 3864, ISO 7010, ISO 7001, ISO 22727, IEC 80416-1, ISO 9186, ANSI Z535, ISO 23601

Full texts are paywalled; numbers below come only from public pages (Wikipedia summaries, standards-body abstracts, a public transcript of ANSI Z535.3-2011, research papers). Where a value was not public it is marked "not public".

### ISO 3864 (safety colours, shapes, symbol design)
- Parts: 1 = design principles for safety signs and markings (layout, colour, sizing); 2 = product safety labels; 3 = design principles for graphical symbols in safety signs; 4 = colorimetric/photometric properties [S52].
- Shape = meaning (as used in ISO 7010): circle with diagonal bar = prohibition (red); circle = mandatory (blue); equilateral triangle with rounded corners = warning (yellow, black); square/rectangle = safe condition (green) and fire equipment (red) [S53].
- Safety colours (Wikipedia summary of ISO 3864-4, RAL-based): warning Signal Yellow RAL 1003; prohibition/fire Signal Red RAL 3001; mandatory Signal Blue RAL 5005; safe condition Signal Green RAL 6032; white RAL 9003; symbol black RAL 9004 [S52]. Risk-level colours (ISO 3864-2:2016): CAUTION yellow, WARNING orange, DANGER red with white [S52].
- Prohibition sign geometry: red band 10 % of the outer diameter; diagonal bar 8 % of the outer diameter, at 45° from upper left to lower right; white outer border 2.5–5 %; red ≥ 35 % of the sign area [S54].
- Safety markings: alternating 45° stripes of safety + contrast colour (yellow/black hazard, red/white fire or prohibition, blue/white mandatory, green/white safe) [S52].
- Sign layout: symbol + optional supplementary text panel, vertical or horizontal; in horizontal layouts the text sits to the right of the symbol; multi-message signs combine several symbol+text pairs; safe-condition and fire signs combine with a direction arrow [S52].
- ISO 3864-3 arrow types: A general movement (60° head), B rotation (60°), C forces/fluids (84°), D movement of people (84–86°, shown at 45° and 90°) [S52].
- ISO 3864-3 purpose: standard principles for visual clarity and consistency, to improve recognition and comprehension; its criteria decide acceptance into ISO 7010; current edition 2024; the 2012 revision changed the human-figure recommendations; line-width numbers not public [S55].
- ISO/TC 145 offers a human-figure template for anyone to use; Geoffrey Peckham (chair of ISO/TC 145 and of the ANSI Z535 committee) says head shape, body form and overall structure stay constant across symbols [S56].

### ISO 7010 (registered safety signs)
- First published 2003 (from ISO 3864:1984 + ISO 6309); current ISO 7010:2019 + amendments; aims at safety information that relies on words as little as possible [S53]. Prefixes E (emergency/safe condition), F (fire), M, P, W; where no specific symbol exists, use the general sign (M001, P001, W001) with supplementary text [S53].

### ISO 7001 and ISO 22727 (public information symbols)
- ISO 7001: 177 symbols in seven categories (accessibility, public facilities, transport, behaviour, commercial, tourism/culture, sport); editions 1980, 1990, 2007, 2023 [S57]. Designed to read without text and to work both on a small floor plan and on a large hanging sign; no colours prescribed, only clear contrast; ISO 3864 safety colours are discouraged (green/white could be mistaken for the ISO 7010 evacuation arrow); negation = red slash (objects) or red cross (behaviours) laid on top, placed with the ISO 22727 template so key elements stay visible [S57].
- ISO 22727:2007 governs the design (and testing) of ISO 7001 symbols; contents include use of template (6.2), line width (6.3) and template layouts with/without negation bar (7); it does not apply to safety signs or road signs; numeric values not public [S58].

### IEC 80416-1:2008 (symbols on equipment, ISO 7000 / IEC 60417)
- The 2008 edition makes the line width of symbol originals mandatory at 2 mm or 4 mm and lets originals sit in the 75 mm square instead of the octagon [S59]. INFERENCE: stroke = 2/75 ≈ 2.7 % (or 5.3 %) of the template side.

### ANSI Z535 (US)
- Parts: Z535.1 colours, .2 facility signs, .3 symbol criteria, .4 product labels, .5 tags/tapes, .6 manuals; signal panels DANGER white on red, WARNING black on orange, CAUTION black on yellow, NOTICE white on blue; colours have tolerances compatible with ISO 3864 [S60].
- ANSI Z535.3-2011 (public transcript): design on a 2.75 in square template (A3.2); 0.04 in suggested as the minimum dimension for symbol elements, i.e. line widths and gaps (A3.2; INFERENCE ≈ 1.45 % of the template); human figure on a grid of equal units (A6.3): 12 units tall, trunk 2 units wide, circular head 1.75 units; hands and feet end in semicircles; fingertips 0.25-unit circles; foreshortened limbs +0.5 unit; solid forms preferred over outline (A5.2–A5.3); simple geometric forms preferred, no superfluous detail or decoration (A3.4); symbols designed as elements of one consistent visual system (A3.1, 6.1); visually consistent arrows (A6.2); surround shapes only for safety-alert and prohibition symbols (5.1); a symbol without demonstrated understanding must be backed by a word message (7.2) [S61].

### Comprehension testing (ISO 9186-1, ANSI Z535.3)
- ISO 9186-1 specifies the method for testing how well a symbol variant communicates its message (paywalled) [S62].
- Acceptance: Gungor (2023) cites 67 % comprehension as the ISO 3864 acceptance criterion and 85 % for ANSI Z535.3-2022 [S63]; ANSI Z535.3-2011 suggests 85 % correct with at most 5 % critical confusions, for a sample of 50 (B1) [S61].

### ISO 23601 (escape and evacuation plans)
- Based on ISO 7010 signs, colours and design requirements; specifies plan size, content, background colours, letter and character size, safety signs, colour codes and scale; the viewer's position, including the correct line of sight, must be evident; the detailed plan is drawn as seen by the viewer; legend + plan metadata (creator, building, floor, date, revision) [S64][S65]. Numeric sizes not public.

### What this means for type + icon consistency
- The standards fix shape, colour and figure/ground; the pictogram inside must respect a minimum stroke/gap tied to the template (ANSI 0.04 in / 2.75 in; IEC 2 mm / 75 mm). INFERENCE: for FrontRooms the minimum stroke of any icon should be ≥ 1/36 of its frame side (≈ 2.75 %), comfortably below a bold sans stem when the label cap height is about 1/3 of the frame (see spec below).
- The human figure is a fixed component (ISO/TC 145, ANSI 12-unit grid); our game-specific signs (EGRESS, FLOOR WARDEN …) should reuse one figure, never redraw it per sign [S56][S61].
- Text is secondary but structural: supplementary text panel to the right (horizontal) or below (vertical), never inside the safety shape [S52]. Signal words (DANGER/WARNING/CAUTION/NOTICE) are a colour-and-type code of their own [S60].

## 10. Modern equivalents · Apple SF Symbols, Google Material Symbols, IBM icons + Plex (Carbon)

### Apple SF Symbols (type-locked symbols)
- Symbols align automatically with text in every weight and size; nine weights from ultralight to black, each matching a San Francisco weight, so symbol and adjacent text match in weight precisely [S66].
- Three scales (small, medium default, large) are defined relative to the cap height of San Francisco; changing scale keeps the weight match with text of the same point size [S66]. Template scale factors: S 0.783, M 1.0, L 1.29 [S67].
- The system centres symbols vertically on San Francisco's cap height in every scale and weight; the template's Guides layer holds an outline capital A per scale as reference glyph; baseline and cap-height guides set the baseline offset [S67].
- 27 variants (9 weights × 3 scales); a variable template needs only Ultralight-S, Regular-S and Black-S (same path count and control points) and the rest are interpolated; each variant can carry its own left/right margin guides, and negative margins help optical horizontal alignment [S67][S66].
- Draw as filled paths: Apple advises converting strokes to paths, which also makes small optical adjustments easier [S67]. Custom symbols should match system symbols in level of detail, optical weight, alignment, position and perspective [S66].
- Rendering modes: monochrome, hierarchical, palette, multicolor; variable colour is for showing change, not depth [S66].

### Google Material Symbols (variable icon font)
- Axes: FILL 0–1; wght 100–700 (default 400); GRAD −50…200 (default 0); opsz 20–48 dp [S68]. Grade can be matched to text that has a grade axis (Google's example: −25); a low grade reduces glare for light symbols on dark backgrounds; optical size adjusts stroke weight automatically as the size changes [S68].
- System-icon grid (Material 1/2): 24 dp icon, 20 × 20 dp live area (2 dp padding), keyline shapes (square 18 dp, circle 20 dp, rectangles 16 × 20 / 20 × 16), 2 dp stroke for all strokes including curves and interior/exterior (1.5 dp only as optical correction), 2 dp exterior corner radius, square interior corners, square terminals, front-facing, whole-pixel coordinates [S69][S70].

### IBM UI icons, pictograms and IBM Plex (the closest model for FrontRooms' Plex Mono)
- UI icons: drawn on a 32 × 32 px pixel grid and scaled down linearly; 2 px padding; one 2 px stroke for all icons for uniformity; 2 px corner radius, increased only in multiples of two; square arrow tips; 45° for even anti-aliasing, otherwise 15° steps; straight-on or profile views only; points snapped to the pixel grid [S71].
- Type bond: IBM states that its icon conventions are meant to bond the icons to IBM Plex and that each icon shares details with the letterforms; the shared details it lists are rounded exteriors with square (90°) interiors, a pointed tip, slab characteristics and square caps; the icons are based on Plex details [S71].
- Pictograms: 32 × 32 px master grid, stroke centres on grid lines, 0.72 px productive / 1.44 px expressive stroke, ≥ 1 px padding, 2 px radius, 45° or 15° steps, no perspective; each pictogram is designed to pair with Plex [S72].
- Pairing with text (Carbon): 16 and 20 px icons are tuned to balance with 14 and 16 px Plex; next to text, icons are centre-aligned, not baseline-aligned; icon colour = text colour, with the same 4.5:1 contrast; keep the icon:text size ratio fixed [S73].
- IBM Plex: Plex Mono glyphs all share a 600-unit width (typewriter heritage); eight weights; IBM presents every detail as deliberate [S74].

### What this means for type + icon consistency
- All three systems tie icons to the face by (a) weight matching, (b) cap-height-relative sizing and centring, (c) shared micro-details (terminals, corners, angles) and (d) per-size optical correction [S66][S67][S68][S71].
- INFERENCE: the IBM detail list is a ready-made checklist for FrontRooms: identify the micro-details of Heros Bold (flat horizontal terminals, near-square counters, sharp 90° corners, no rounding) and of Plex Mono (slab serifs on i/l/r, angled cuts), and give print icons Heros details and CRT/meta icons Plex details.
- INFERENCE: Apple's three-master interpolation (light, regular, black) is the efficient way to build a weight-matched icon set for Bayon (one heavy condensed weight), Heros Bold and Plex Mono Regular: draw masters, interpolate, then correct optically per size.

### 11.1 Checklist (each rule with the sources that support it)
1. Stroke = stem. Icon line weight equals the vertical stem of the face it sits with, at the size shown: SF Symbols weight matching [S66]; IBM 2 px stroke tied to Plex [S71]; DOT's one optically consistent line weight + Helvetica Medium chosen for compatibility with the symbols [S22]; UK layouts measured in letter stroke widths [S51]. Horizontal strokes follow the face's own h/v contrast (Heros Bold crossbar = 0.83 × stem; Heros Cn Bold and Bayon = 1.0; M01).
2. Size from cap height. Inline icons are sized and centred on the cap height: SF scales 0.783 / 1.0 / 1.29 relative to cap height, centred on it [S67]; Carbon pairs 16/20 px icons with 14/16 px Plex [S73]; Font Awesome icons inherit the text's font size [S78]. Sign pictograms: letter height = 1/4–2/5 of the symbol (DOT) [S22]; symbol = 14 stroke widths = 3.5 x-heights (UK drawing) [S51].
3. Optical sizes. Redraw per size, do not just scale: Arntz cut a separate linocut per size [S17]; Material's opsz axis adjusts stroke with size [S68]; SF Symbols ships 27 drawn/interpolated variants [S67]; Material allows 1.5 dp strokes as small-size correction [S69]; Frutiger judged Roissy too light and too tight [S27].
4. Terminals and corners from the letterforms. IBM icons share Plex details: rounded exteriors with 90° interiors, pointed tips, slab, square caps [S71]; Material: square terminals, square interior corners, 2 dp exterior radius [S69]; Wyman's rounded-corner frames [S12][S14]; DOT softened contours [S22]; Aicher redrew Univers with round terminals for TV blur [S07].
5. One angle system. 0/45/90° (Aicher [S03][S04]; Crouwel [S43][S44]); IBM 45° for clean anti-aliasing, else 15° steps [S71]; ISO prohibition bar at 45° and 45° safety stripes [S54][S52]. Keep the perpendicular thickness constant on diagonals (Holt's correction of Munich) [S01].
6. Counters and gaps. Minimum gap = minimum stroke: ANSI minimum element 0.04 in on a 2.75 in template for lines and spaces [S61]; uniform gaps (Holt) [S01]; counters balanced against spacing (Ruder) [S38]; avoid very thin lines that anti-alias to a faint colour (WCAG) [S75].
7. Spacing in type units. DOT layout grid: lettering, arrow and neighbouring symbols spaced in fractions of the symbol width [S22]; UK: word space 2.5 sw, border 1.5 sw [S51]; NYCTA: grid unit = cap height, fixed letter/word spacing units [S33][S36]; Müller-Brockmann: gaps = 1–2 text lines, images span whole fields [S37].
8. Alignment. Centre inline icons on the cap-height band, not the baseline (Apple [S67], Carbon [S73]); ISO horizontal layout puts text right of the safety sign [S52]; the arrow keeps a constant position relative to symbols and sits on the side it points to (DOT) [S22]; fixed slot order on a sign (Wyman's M + icon + arrow) [S14].
9. One level of abstraction. Least possible detail (Neurath) [S15]; simplification is what makes the set coherent (DOT) [S22]; simple geometric forms, no superfluous detail, solid rather than outline (ANSI) [S61]; match level of detail, optical weight, alignment, position and perspective (Apple) [S66]; one human figure as a constant (ISO/TC 145) [S56], ANSI 12-unit figure [S61]; compound signs from a base + modifier (Arntz) [S17].
10. Colour as code, with redundancy. Shape and colour both carry the class (ISO) [S53]; few colours with fixed meanings and a black-and-white fallback (Neurath: seven colours, B/W must work) [S15]; figure black on white, never reversed; red only for a closed list of messages (DOT) [S22]; icon colour = text colour, 4.5:1 like text (Carbon) [S73]; informative icons ≥ 3:1 (WCAG 1.4.11) [S75]; ISO 3864 colours kept for safety, not for general information (ISO 7001) [S57]; colour not used for depth (Apple) [S66].
11. Polarity compensation. Light-on-dark needs a lighter weight and looser spacing: Transport Medium (white on dark) vs Heavy (black on white) [S50]; a low grade for light symbols on dark (Material) [S68]; more letterspacing for white lettering (DOT) [S22].
12. Condensed display faces. Bayon is a Khmer display face for headlines, titles and banners [S79] (FrontRooms uses its Latin caps). Pictograms stay square and pair natively with normal-width sign faces; condensed cuts are space-savers (DIN 1451 Engschrift; Linotype's Vialog note on pictograms) [S76]. INFERENCE: next to Bayon (O width/height 0.55) match stroke (0.17 cap) and height (cap height), keep the icon square, and give it Bayon's own side bearings (≈ 0.06 cap per side; measured H side bearing 81 / cap 1462) so it reads as a wide letter, not a foreign object.
13. Programme, not drawings. Write the rules so new icons can be derived (Gerstner: structures from which single solutions are derived) [S40]; derive the grid from the type (Müller-Brockmann, NYCTA) [S37][S33]; consistency as the grammar of a visual language (Kinneir) [S49].
14. Test comprehension. ISO 3864 practice accepts at 67 %, ANSI Z535.3 at 85 % with < 5 % critical confusion [S63][S61]; ISO 9186-1 method [S62]; an untested symbol is backed by a word message [S61].

## A consistency spec for FrontRooms

Fifteen rules a designer can apply. "s" = the stroke unit of the face the icon sits with (measured, §11.0). "F" = side of a square pictogram field. "cap" = cap height of the adjacent label. Numbers marked INFERENCE are my derivations from the sources and measurements, not quoted rules; check them in a side-by-side proof at one scale before locking.

1. **Stroke unit = the label's stem.** s = 0.206 cap next to Heros Bold, 0.171 cap next to Heros Cn Bold, 0.170 cap next to Bayon (HUD), 0.120 cap next to Plex Mono (CRT/meta), 0.211 cap next to Courier Prime Bold key numerals. Every line element of an icon is 1 s; nothing in an icon is thinner than 1 s except hairline details that also exist in the face. Sources: SF weight matching [S66]; IBM 2 px tied to Plex [S71]; DOT one optical line weight + Helvetica Medium chosen for compatibility with the symbols [S22]; UK stroke-width unit [S51]; measurements M01.
2. **Mirror the face's own stroke contrast.** Horizontal strokes are 0.83 s in Heros Bold contexts (crossbar 125 / stem 150) and 1.0 s in Heros Cn Bold, Bayon and CRT contexts (monoline). Curves thin at top/bottom like the face's O (Heros Bold O thin/thick 0.85). Sources: M01; Univers' thick/thin modulation [S29]; Apple's optical-weight matching [S66].
3. **Inline icons live on the cap-height band.** Three sizes, all centred vertically on the band between baseline and cap line: S = 1.0 cap (icon inside a line of caps, e.g. Bayon HUD), M ≈ 1.28 cap (icon leading a label), L ≈ 1.65 cap (standalone with label). Round shapes overshoot like O (≈ 1.6–1.7 % of cap top and bottom: Heros Bold 12/729, Plex 12/698). Never baseline-align. Sources: SF scales 0.783/1.0/1.29 relative to cap height, centred on it [S67]; Carbon 16/20 px icons with 14/16 px Plex, centre-aligned [S73]; INFERENCE for the 1.0/1.28/1.65 mapping.
4. **Sign pictograms use a 14 s field.** F = 14 s (UK symbols are 14 stroke widths high [S51]); then the label's cap height is 0.35 F with Heros Bold and 0.42 F with Heros Cn Bold or Bayon, which lands inside DOT's illustrated letter-to-symbol ratios 1/3–2/5 [S22] (INFERENCE: derived from M01). The resulting stroke ≈ 7 % of F sits just above IEC 80416-1's 2–4 mm lines on a 75 mm pattern (2.7–5.3 %) [S59] and far above ANSI's minimum element of 0.04 in on 2.75 in (1.45 %) [S61].
5. **Draw on the s-grid.** 14 × 14 s field, 1 s padding, 12 × 12 s live area; snap to half units (28 × 28 grid) for centring; counters and gaps ≥ 1 s (never below the stroke). The ANSI human figure (12 units tall) fills the live area exactly if 1 unit = 1 s (INFERENCE). Sources: Müller-Brockmann fields derived from type [S37]; NYCTA grid = cap height [S33]; Material/IBM padded live areas [S69][S71]; ANSI minimum element for lines and gaps [S61]; Holt's uniform gaps [S01].
6. **One angle system: 0°, 45°, 90°.** Diagonals keep the same perpendicular thickness s (do not build them from grid cells; Holt's correction of Munich) [S01]. Arrows for game wayfinding use 45° flanks (90° head) and a 1 s shaft (INFERENCE), optically corrected like the NYCTA arrow (4° on the diagonal bar) [S36]; on ISO safety/evacuation signs use the ISO 3864-3 arrow (type D, 84–86° head, for movement of people) [S52]. Sources: Aicher 45/90 [S03][S04]; Crouwel 0/45/90 [S43][S44]; IBM 45° [S71]; ISO 45° bar and stripes [S54][S52].
7. **Terminals and corners follow the medium's face** (Ruder's closest formal match between type and picture) [S38]. Print and plates (Heros): flat cuts square to the stroke, sharp 90° corners, no corner radius, curved strokes ending on horizontal/vertical cuts like Helvetica's C/S (INFERENCE from the letterforms). HUD (Bayon): monoline, square caps. CRT (Plex Mono on the screen raster): pixel-grid strokes, 45° chamfers instead of small curves (New Alphabet [S43]), slab-like square caps (IBM's Plex details [S71]). Sources: IBM shared details [S71]; Material square terminals [S69]; Aicher's medium-specific round terminals for TV [S07].
8. **Optical masters, not scaling.** Draw at least four masters: CRT raster, HUD (≤ 32 px), in-world plate texture, small print (forms, placards, evacuation plans). Small masters drop details, open gaps to ≥ 1 s and never render a stroke below 1 device pixel (Bayon at 16 px font size: cap ≈ 11.4 px → s ≈ 2 px; INFERENCE from M01). Sources: Arntz's separate linocut per size [S17]; Material optical size [S68]; SF variants per weight/scale [S67]; Frutiger's own correction of Roissy (heavier, looser) [S27].
9. **Spacing in s units.** Inline gap between icon and label = 2 s (≈ one word space of the face: Heros Bold 0.381 cap = 1.85 s, Bayon 0.364 cap = 2.14 s; UK word space 2.5 sw ≈ 2.25 s in Heros Bold terms; INFERENCE). On plates follow the DOT grid in F: ¼ F between pictograms, ½ F between arrow and pictogram, lettering at one symbol width where the panel grid allows [S22]; panel border/keyline 1.5 s (UK border 1.5 sw) [S51]. Sources: DOT [S22]; UK [S51]; NYCTA unit spacing [S36].
10. **Fixed slots and alignment on signs.** Horizontal ISO layout: safety sign left, supplementary text right [S52]; arrow on the side it points to, in a constant position relative to the symbols [S22]; one slot order per sign family, like Wyman's M + station icon + arrow [S14]. Inline: icon before label, centred on the cap band (rule 3).
11. **One human figure for every sign.** A single figure kit (ISO/TC 145 template logic: head, body and structure constant [S56]) drawn on the ANSI grid in s units: 12 u tall, trunk 2 u, head 1.75 u circle, hands/feet ending in semicircles, poses from fixed pivot points [S61]; head separated from body and no faces (Munich [S04]); new meanings by modifiers on the base (Arntz's compound signs [S17]).
12. **One level of abstraction.** Solid silhouettes plus 1 s line elements; front or profile views only, no perspective; no texture, no internal detail smaller than 1 s; reuse existing ISO/ANSI ideograms for standard meanings and invent only game-only concepts. Sources: Neurath's least-possible-detail rule [S15]; DOT simplification = coherence [S22]; ANSI simple forms, solid over outline [S61]; IBM/Material no perspective [S71][S69]; Frutiger on conventional ideograms [S30].
13. **Colour is a code carried by panels, with redundancy.** Pictograms are one colour: black ink (on white or canary paper, consistent with the existing print rule that yellow is the paper and ink stays black) or white (on safety-colour panels and CRT black). ISO meanings are reserved: red = prohibition/fire/DANGER, blue = mandatory, yellow = warning/CAUTION, orange = WARNING, green = safe condition/egress [S52][S53][S60]; game-only meanings never borrow these colours [S57]. Every colour-coded difference must also differ by shape or word, and every icon must pass a one-colour print test (Neurath's B/W rule) [S15]. Contrast: informative icons ≥ 3:1, text ≥ 4.5:1 [S75][S73]; never use colour for depth [S66].
14. **Polarity masters.** Reversed icons (white on red/green/blue panels or on the CRT) use a lighter master, one step down: the Heros Cn Bold ratio 0.171 instead of Heros Bold 0.206 (−17 %; INFERENCE from M01), and reversed lettering gets extra tracking. Sources: Transport Medium (light on dark) vs Heavy (dark on light) [S50]; Material low grade on dark [S68]; DOT's extra letterspacing for white on dark [S22].
15. **A programme with a comprehension gate.** Keep these rules as a parametric spec (s, F, grid, angles, masters) so new icons are derived, not invented [S40][S49]; every new icon is checked with playtesters and passes ≥ 67 % correct (ISO 3864 practice), aiming for 85 % with < 5 % critical confusion (ANSI Z535.3); below that it ships with a word label [S63][S61][S62].

## Gaps and cautions
- Not public (paywalled) and therefore not used: numeric line-width and gap rules of ISO 3864-3 and ISO 22727, symbol/text sizes of ISO 23601, and the full text of Frutiger's Signs and Symbols. The ANSI Z535.3 numbers come from the 2011 edition (public transcript); the 2022 edition may differ.
- The UK stroke-width figures (4 sw = x-height; symbols 14 sw high; word space 2.5 sw; border 1.5 sw) come from search snippets of a Transport Scotland working drawing and a Transport Malta manual, not from the UK Traffic Signs Manual text itself.
- The DOT spacing figure (one symbol width between lettering and symbol) comes from OCR without seeing the illustration; check the scan (archive.org pb-239352) before adopting it.
- The Munich grid is partly a myth: Holt and FH Potsdam both show the published pictograms do not sit on one strict template grid [S01][S04]. Use Munich for the principle (one kit, 0/45/90), not for measurements.
- DIN 1450 ratios come from a public mailing-list quotation whose column mapping is unclear [S77].
- All INFERENCE numbers in the spec (1.0/1.28/1.65 cap, 14 s field, 2 s gap, −17 % reversed) should be proofed side by side at one scale in Figma before they become rules.
