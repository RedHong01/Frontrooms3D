## 0. The four findings that change the design

1. **The station TV's content is the biggest IP risk, more than the desk.**
   - A24's *Backrooms* (2026) puts its doorway in a furniture showroom's basement and dates its prologue to June 1990 [read R55, R56].
   - Its viral campaign was a 30 s retro local furniture-store TV spot [read R57].
   - Its trailer shows rows of identical showroom armchairs (97 s) [project R60].
   - Red has already decided A24 option A: the FRONT ROOMS FURNITURE ad stays, in-game only (10-22, 广告's chat). So the station room must not add a second overlap: **no rows of identical upholstered armchairs facing the TV**, and no showroom staging (§3.8).
2. **Kane Pixels owns 1990 night surveillance:** timestamped motion-camera logs, black-and-white corridor CCTV, a red-alarm control room, a taped broadcast interrupted by an ad, "NO SIGNAL" beside a VCR [read R53]. Our screens stay **a live broadcast on a CRT**: no recorder at the desk, no date stamp, no timecode, never "NO SIGNAL".
3. **FNAF owns "the guard sits in one room and watches monitors"** [read R64]. Our station is a room you **walk into and take from**, never a post you sit at. This fits V7 (the live monitor beat) not being chosen.
4. **Four patterns recur, and they are ours to borrow as principles:**
   - the watcher watches the wrong screen;
   - the broadcast outlives its operator;
   - sign-off and snow are the hinge into the uncanny;
   - the TV being on or off steers what the creature does.

## 2. The shared grammar: the empty post with the set still on

Across these works the empty post is a portrait of an absent person drawn in light.
- **The guard is defined by what he left.** The chair faces the screen, not the door. There is a cup, an open log, and a key nobody signed out.
- **The screen is the last occupant.** It speaks to nobody, so whoever walks in becomes its audience.
- **The broadcast keeps time.** Its sign-off is where normal ends.
- **Its being on is a truce.** When it stops, something comes.

FrontRooms borrows only these principles:
- absence told through props;
- the voice before the light;
- attention facing the wrong way;
- a broadcast that keeps time;
- a switch-off that breaks the truce.

## 3. Design implications (feed `10_directions.md` and `20_layout.md`)

1. **One lit screen, one voice.**
   - The station TV is the floor's only live broadcast and the room's key light (V2/V3).
   - Its sound reaches the doorways before its light.
2. **Tell the absent guard with 4–6 props, never a body:**
   - the chair pushed back and turned to the TV;
   - a cup;
   - the open sign-in log;
   - a jacket on the chair back;
   - an empty radio charger, or the watchclock's empty hook.
   - Several are kit gaps (`05_visual_answers.md` §2).
3. **The counter stands between the approach and the key.** To take the key the player steps behind the desk, so the transgression is spatial. From behind the desk both ways out should be visible.
4. **The TV faces the guard's chair, not the entrance.**
   - On approach the player sees the screen's moving spill on the walls and ceiling first, then the screen once they stand where the guard sat.
   - The reveal rewards walking in.
5. **The broadcast is the clock.**
   - The night runs: ad, then ID, then sign-off, then bars, then snow.
   - Never "NO SIGNAL": it is a digital or VCR overlay, anachronistic for a 1990 set, and a Kane Pixels image.
   - (An optional idea, §4.)
6. **Switching off is the alarm.**
   - The take kills the set (V2's collapse), and the room drops to its one lit entrance lamp.
   - It is one readable change, within photosafety.
7. **Monitors are dressing, never a minigame.** If the Office variant has a CCTV monitor, it shows one still view of the corridor the player came from. It is never switchable.
8. **The waiting variant is institutional, not a showroom.**
   - Ganged tandem seating on a steel beam, an ashtray stand, a magazine rack, a wall clock.
   - The TV high on a cart or a wall bracket.
   - A counter with a transaction top or a sliding window.
   - Never rows of identical upholstered armchairs (A24, R60). `01_research.md` confirms the period forms.
9. **Paper, not playback.** The guard's voice is paper: the log, the posted orders, the key ledger, the watchclock route card. No recorded predecessor (FNAF) and no rule-manual gag (*Night at the Museum*).
10. **False occupancy is the first scare, kept readable.** From the doorway, a jacket on the chair lit by the set can read as "someone's here". Up close the emptiness lands. The room is never hidden in darkness (dark is never cover).
11. **Name away from the canon.**
    - In-world signs never say Lobby, Threshold or Station. Those are the Backrooms wiki's Level 0 names and Escape the Backrooms' sign [read R84, R85; search R83].
    - The player-facing name stays `SECURITY DESK`.
    - **Flag for Red:** the HUD's start-room text `LEVEL 0 / THE LOBBY` matches the Fandom wiki's "Level 0: The Lobby" [search R87]. See `20_layout.md` §7.

## 4. One optional idea from the references (for the directions; not proposed for Phase 1)

**"The night goes on as you go down."** Each floor's station set could show a later point in the same broadcast night:

| Floor | Station TV |
|---|---|
| 1 | the ad loop (as approved) |
| B1 | the late movie or a PSA |
| B2 | Thought For Today and the sign-off |
| B3 | bars, with the clock reading later each floor |
| deeper | snow |

- It turns the descent into time passing, with no HUD.
- **Costs and conflicts:**
  - the beacon's tune (taught on the title TV) would only hold on Floor 1;
  - the bars' 1 kHz tone collides with "the lock-on chime is the only tonal sound", so the bars would have to be silent or hiss;
  - 声音设计, 广告 and 教程设计 would all need to agree.
- If it is ever wanted, it is Phase 2, and every state keeps a broadcast sound family distinct from the hunter's.
